# Sell silent mornings to the woken partner

The strongest buyer for the Silent Wake Band isn't the heavy sleeper. It's **the person lying awake while someone else's alarm goes off and they sleep on**. That person writes almost all of the angry posts ("a level of disrespect I can't explain"), and they often end up buying a quiet alarm for their partner. The behavior is already common. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) reports that **15% of US adults already use a silent alarm so they don't wake a bed partner**, and **31% have slept apart** to accommodate one ([AASM 2025](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/sleep-prioritization-survey-2025-adjusting-your-sleep-routine.pdf)). At $39.99 the band matches its two closest rivals on price: the Homedics WakeBand ($39.99, needs an app) and FitSleeps ($39–$49, with a 100-day trial). The $64.99 two-pack works out to $32.50 a band, and no competitor 2-pack turned up. On Meta, competitors push "heavy sleeper", "world's most powerful alarm" and "70% off". No competitor ad found led with the couples angle, though the Ad Library search was noisy, so treat that as a likely gap rather than a confirmed one. The risk that decides sales is the category's most common 1-star review: **"I slept through it."** Don't promise to wake anyone through anything. Get the vibration, repeat and battery specs from the supplier, test the band yourself, and use the 30-day return policy as the safety net. Test four angles first: the woken partner, the considerate early riser, the gift for the snoozer (2-pack), and don't wake the baby.

## Compliance guardrails for Meta and the FTC

Meta's **personal attributes** policy bans ads that "assert or imply" the viewer has a health condition, disability or other personal attribute, including through "you/your" phrasing ([Meta policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/), summary only; the page couldn't be opened). Blogs report that Meta rewrote its health and wellness rules around July 22, 2026, but this wasn't confirmed on Meta's own pages ([Adligator](https://adligator.com/blog/meta-health-wellness-ad-policy-update-2026)). The FTC's 2024 rule bans fake, AI-written, bought and undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties of up to **$51,744 per violation** at the 2024 rate ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), [DWT](https://dwt.com/insights/2024/08/ftc-finalizes-rule-banning-fake-consumer-reviews)). The FTC's pricing guides say a "was" price must be a genuine former price offered for a reasonably substantial period ([16 CFR 233](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)).

## Conclusion

The research turns the usual pitch for this category around. Competitors sell power to heavy sleepers, who are the most doubtful buyers and the most likely to leave a "slept through it" review. Your slogan, "Wake up. Let them sleep.", is aimed at the more motivated person in the room: the one who gets woken, who is angry about it, and who is already willing to buy a quiet alarm for someone else. The 2-pack and gift season make that buyer easy to reach right now. The early riser is the second buyer: considerate, slightly guilty, and often already using a silent alarm.

The weak point is proof, not demand. Every rival is judged on whether the buzz is strong enough to wake its owner and quiet enough not to wake anyone else. The evidence here can't tell you whether your band clears that bar. Your supplier's specs and your own bedroom test can. Until then, sell the situation, not superlatives, and let the 30-day returns carry the risk.
