# Cheaper impressions alone won't rescue this campaign

**Yes, both numbers are abnormal.** Your CPM (the cost of 1,000 ad views) of **£54.32, about $70**, is roughly three to four and a half times the published US e-commerce medians and about double what mid-November should cost. **£3.52 per link click** is three to eight times the published figures. Your ads are being clicked at a normal rate (1.54% of views), so the click price is high only because the views are expensive. The evidence points to four causes, strongest first. (1) Meta is spending most of your money on its dearest audience: women over 45 took 54% of spend at about £71 per 1,000 views, mostly in the Facebook feed. (2) The campaign has 2 purchases to learn from and £35 a day spread over 10 ads, so Meta is guessing who buys. (3) The run-up to Black Friday adds perhaps 15 to 25%. (4) Your ads are all still images, which sit in the expensive feed and do badly in the video slots. The harder truth is the money: you make about **£22 on a single band and £35 on a 2-pack** before ads, each sale is costing **£63**, and even normal-priced views would not close that gap unless your store converts several times better than a typical one. My recommendation is to stop buying views through Black Friday week, fix the store's trust problem, relaunch a tighter test in early December with a fixed pot of money, and let that plus a January test decide whether Meta works at $39.99. Two purchases prove nothing about who buys, and most outside figures here were read as search-result excerpts, not checked on the live page.

> **How to read the numbers (read once).** Everything labelled "your account" was read directly from your Meta ad account and Shopify on 19 Nov 2026 and is the strongest evidence here. Meta's own Help Center pages were read in full. **Every other outside figure (benchmarks, market data, competitor prices) was seen only as a search-result excerpt because the pages could not be opened, so treat those as unverified.** Dollar figures are converted at **£1 = $1.29**, the rate implied by your own billing; it was not looked up. No November 2026 benchmark data exists yet, so comparisons use November 2025 and 2026 year-to-date.

## Four causes, ranked by how much evidence sits behind each

Meta runs an auction for every single ad view. It says the winner is the ad with the highest "total value", made of the advertiser's bid, the predicted chance that this person does what the advertiser wants, and ad quality ([Meta: About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)). You set no bid; Meta bids for you to spend the daily budget, and because you asked for purchases it bids high only for people it predicts will buy ([Meta: About bid cap](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/272503946776144)). Those people are wanted by every other store, so they cost more. That mechanism explains the pattern in your account.

**Cause 1 is the best-evidenced because it is visible in your own numbers.** Where Meta showed your ads to cheaper people, the price was £38 to £43 per 1,000 views: men, under-45s, the "R3 Whiteboard" ad (£39), and the partial day on 19 Nov (£38). Where it chased the people it predicted would buy, the price doubled: women 65+ cost £88, and 17 Nov, the day both sales came in, was the dearest day at £71. The dearer views were also the ones that clicked. Women 45+ clicked 2.4% of the time and cost £2.98 a click, against 0.5% and about £9.50 a click for under-45s. Your four busiest ads show the same thing: their CPMs range from £39 to £81, yet each cost between £3.00 and £3.34 per click. So a high CPM here is partly Meta paying more for better prospects, which is why one widely followed independent Meta ads specialist says CPM is not a goal in itself and a high one "isn't necessarily bad" ([Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/meta-ads-performance-cpm/), excerpt only).

**Cause 2 is what turns a fair premium into a bad deal.** Paying buyer prices only works if Meta's guess about who buys is good, and with 2 purchases on record it is close to a blind guess. Reaching Meta's 50 purchases a week on £245 a week would need each sale to cost £4.90, which is impossible, so the campaign stays in what Meta calls "learning limited" at any budget you could sensibly afford ([Meta: learning limited](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/269269737396981)). Meta calls that "not a penalty", and nothing it publishes describes a surcharge on new accounts.

Several suspects can be cleared. Your audience is not too narrow: it is as broad as Meta recommends, and the account scores 98 out of 100 on Meta's own setup check. Your ads are not failing to get clicks. People are not seeing the ads too often overall (1.65 times each), though 91 men aged 55-64 saw them about four times each. One thing stays unknown: Meta's quality ratings for your ads are not available yet, so a low quality score cannot be ruled in or out.

## You need a sale for £22 to £35; you are paying £63

Using your store's figures, profit before ads is $28.45 (£22) on one band and $44.58 (£35) on a 2-pack. **That profit is the most a sale can cost before you lose money.** This campaign spent £126.63 to bring in two orders worth $107.97 (£84), one of which looks like a 2-pack plus shipping protection. Those orders carry about £57 of profit before ads, so the four days lost roughly **£70**, on top of about £280 lost in earlier tests.

Lowering CPM alone does not fix this. The table shows what a sale costs at different view prices and different shares of clickers who buy, holding your click rate at 1.54%.

| CPM | If 1.5% of clickers buy (typical) | If 3% buy (very good store) | If 5.6% buy (your 2 in 36) |
|---|---|---|---|
| £54 (now) | £235 | £118 | £63 |
| £40 | £173 | £87 | £47 |
| £27 (my estimate of normal for this week) | £117 | £58 | £32 |
| £17 (health and wellness median) | £75 | £38 | £20 |

Only the bottom-right corner sits under your £22 to £35 limit. At the typical rate of about 1.5% of clickers buying ([Triple Whale](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks)), you would need views at £5 to £8 per 1,000, a price that does not exist for US buyers. Your own rate of 2 sales from 36 clicks is encouraging, since an average store would expect about half a sale from that many clicks, but a sample that small fits a true rate anywhere from under 1% to over 15%. Paid-social visitors to Shopify stores are reported to convert at about 0.9% ([EasySell](https://easysellapp.com/blogs/wiki/shopify-conversion-rate-benchmarks-2026-by-industry-ecommerce)).

The benchmarks say the same thing from the other direction. The median brand selling orders under $60 pays about $43 (£33) per Meta purchase and roughly breaks even on ad spend before product cost ([MHI Growth Engine](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/meta-ads-benchmarks-by-aov-2026/)). So **even a normal result loses money on a single $39.99 band** and only works when most orders are 2-packs: to cover a £33 cost per sale, about 90% of orders would need to be 2-packs; at £30, about 65%. An earlier estimate in this project put your ad cost near £36 a sale; this campaign's real figure of £63 is worse than that, and about double the median.

## The account is finding women over 45 in the Facebook feed

The market research says the biggest group is couples with different wake times: about 73 million US couple households by the researchers' arithmetic from Census figures ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/families-and-living-arrangements.html)), and 15% of adults already use a silent alarm for a bed partner, peaking at 20 to 21% among 25 to 44 year olds ([AASM 2025 survey](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/sleep-prioritization-survey-2025-adjusting-your-sleep-routine.pdf), the one outside figure here checked against the original). The earlier research in this project concluded that the buyer is often the partner being woken, who buys the band for the other person.

Your account agrees on the "woman buying" part and disagrees on age. **Women 45+ produced 23 of 36 link clicks, and all 3 add-to-carts and both purchases came from women 65+ in the Facebook feed.** Under-45s, the age group the survey data favours, took about £28 and produced 3 clicks. There is a sensible reason older buyers respond: 37% of US adults own a smartwatch, and nearly half of those under 50 do ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/just-under-half-of-adults-under-50-have-a-smartwatch-while-smaller-shares-of-older-adults-say-so/)), so younger viewers more often already have a silent alarm on their wrist for free. It is also gift season, and the Facebook feed skews older.

Hold that loosely. Two purchases and three carts from 182 views could be one or two people. With no purchase history, Meta may simply be falling back on the people who generally buy from Facebook ads, which says little about this product. And the ads built on the angles the earlier research ranked highest, the 2-pack gift and the early-rising parent, got 3 to 6 views each, so they are untested, not beaten.

My recommendation is to **aim with the ads, not with the targeting settings.** Write and shoot for a woman in her 50s or 60s buying for the person whose alarm wakes her, in the Facebook feed, and leave the audience broad. Meta has already moved 77% of spend to over-45s on its own, and its guidance is that narrowing an audience raises costs ([Meta: reducing cost per result](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/321695409726523)). Ads should describe the situation and never suggest the viewer is old or hard of hearing, which Meta's rules on personal attributes bar ([Meta policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/personal-attributes)).

On whether paid social can work at this price, the market evidence is discouraging. The best-selling vibrating watch on Amazon sells at $23.88 (£18.50) and an estimated 3,000 to 4,000 units a month ([AsinSight](https://www.asinsight.com/report/US/vibrating-watch), a modelled estimate), with next-day delivery against your 7 to 12 days. An ad that teaches someone the product exists also teaches them what to search for. There is no repeat purchase, so the first order has to pay for itself. **At $39.99 for one band, cold Meta traffic is more likely than not to lose money per order even at normal costs.** It becomes workable only if at least two of these hold: a cost per sale near £22 to £28, most orders being 2-packs, or a real share of sales from cheaper sources.

If you would rather cut the budget than pause, that is a weaker version of row 1: it loses less per day, but Meta says a large budget change can itself restart learning, and a smaller budget learns even more slowly.

Some popular fixes are worth declining. Asking Meta for clicks, page views or engagement instead of purchases would cut CPM sharply and look like progress, but Meta then chases whoever clicks cheaply; your 9 Audience Network clicks for £1.95 with no carts are a preview ([Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/common-ad-set-optimization-mistakes/)). Meta does suggest switching from purchases to add-to-cart for low-volume accounts, but at 3 carts in four days that would not settle either. Excluding placements is not worth it: Audience Network cost you £1.95, and Meta's experiment across 147,000 campaigns found all placements gave 11.7% lower cost per result ([Meta: Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/196554084569964)). Raising the budget tends to raise cost per sale because Meta buys the cheapest opportunities first. A cost cap has no stable figure to anchor to and would most likely stop delivery. Fake countdowns, invented "was" prices and made-up scarcity are off the table for legal reasons, and Meta says bait of that kind lowers ad quality anyway.

## Conclusion

The question was why the views cost so much, and the answer changes what is worth worrying about. On my reading of your breakdowns, about half the excess is Meta deliberately paying up for the people it thinks will buy, and those people did click and did buy. The other half is the price of being a tiny new advertiser in the dearest fortnight of the year. Neither is a setup mistake, which is why Meta's own checker scores the account 98 out of 100 and has one modest suggestion.

The number that decides the store's future is cost per sale against a £22 to £35 ceiling, and that depends more on the store and the order size than on the auction. The campaign's most useful result is a hint the earlier research did not predict: the person who responded was an older woman in the Facebook feed, probably buying for someone else. A December test built around her and around the 2-pack, on a page with honest proof, is the cheapest way to find out whether that hint is real. If it is not, the evidence says to keep the $39.99 band and change the channel.
