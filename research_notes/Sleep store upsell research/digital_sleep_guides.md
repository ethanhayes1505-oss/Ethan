Research date: 2026-11-17. Evidence labels used throughout:

- **[VERIFIED]** = I read it on the brand's or organisation's own page or public Shopify catalog feed (`/products.json`) on 2026-11-17.
- **[SNIPPET]** = taken from a web-search result summary; the page itself could not be opened from this session (most hosts were blocked by the session's network allowlist, including zipify.com, samcart.com, focus-digital.co, intelligems.io, cdc.gov, sleepfoundation.org, sleepeducation.org, hostagetape.com, pavlok.com, dodow.io, morphee.co, web.archive.org). Reachable and read directly: fitsleeps.com, mantasleep.com, byloftie.com, apolloneuro.com, aasm.org.

## 1. Which named sleep/wellness/gadget DTC brands sell or bundle digital guides, ebooks, audio or courses with a physical product?

### Takeaway
Among the brands that could be checked, the digital item is almost always a **free bonus or an app/subscription**, not a small paid PDF add-on: FitSleeps lists two $0 guided-breathwork items, Manta gives a "Free Napping Guide" with a stated $18 value on a bundle landing page, supplement funnels give free ebooks with multi-bottle packs, and gadget brands (Hatch, Apollo, Loftie) attach audio libraries or memberships. I found **no named sleep brand selling a ~$5-10 PDF guide as a tick-box add-on** on a product page; the closest price analogs at the direct competitor are non-digital "virtual" add-ons ($7.95 waterproof upgrade, $2.95 shipping insurance, $18 warranty).

## 2. What price points are common for a digital order bump next to a $30-60 product, and what take rates do practitioners report?

### Takeaway
Practitioner rules of thumb put a bump at roughly 10-20% of the main price or in a $5-$15 band, so $6.99 on $39.99 (17.5%) sits inside the guidance. Published take rates range from about 4-16% (Shopify post-purchase data from Zipify) to 25-40% (checkout-software vendors selling to info-product creators); almost all are vendor marketing figures, none is specific to a PDF guide beside a physical gadget, and none comes from a store with near-zero traffic.

### Cited Findings
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] Zipify (OneClickUpsell): merchants average a 16.2% take rate on post-purchase offers; Zipify says benchmarks come from its internal dataset of 15,000+ Shopify stores; "underoptimized" stores sit around 4%. Measured as share of buyers accepting a post-purchase one-click offer, not a product-page checkbox. — [Zipify 2026 upsell playbook](https://zipify.com/blog-how-to-upsell-in-2026/); [Zipify post-purchase guide, updated July 2026](https://zipify.com/blog-post-purchase-upsells-shopify-2026/)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET, 2022] Zipify's own 2022 performance report (from $156M in sales) gave lower averages: 9.9% for post-purchase offers and 3.1% for thank-you-page offers. — [Zipify 2022 Year in Review](https://zipify.com/blog-2022-year-in-review/)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] Upsella (one-click upsell vendor): average post-purchase conversion 4-10%, top performers 15-25%. — [Upsella](https://upsella-com.pages.dev/blog/education/one-click-upsells-explained)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] SamCart: "Order bumps lift AOV 31% on average and convert at 35 to 40%." SamCart's customers are mostly digital-product creators; methodology not shown. — [SamCart blog](https://www.samcart.com/blog/how-to-increase-average-order-value-(7-proven-ways))
- [THIRD-PARTY REPORT, SNIPPET, methodology not described] Focus Digital "average upsell conversion rate" reports list order bumps at 37.8% average conversion; the 2026 edition gives information products 31.2% average and 47.8% top quartile. Launchtip repeats the 37.8% figure. — [Focus Digital 2025](https://focus-digital.co/average-upsell-conversion-rate-2025-report/); [Focus Digital 2026](https://focus-digital.co/average-upsell-conversion-rate-2026-report/); [Launchtip](https://www.launchtip.com/blog/upsell-statistics-in-e-commerce-key-statistics-and-data)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] JVZoo (digital-product marketplace): users see take rates around 30% for bumps priced $10-$27. — [JVZoo blog](https://blog.jvzoo.com/?p=5428)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] Checkout Page: take rates on well-matched bumps of 15-30%. — [Checkout Page](https://checkoutpage.com/blog/upselling-strategies)
- [PRACTITIONER, SNIPPET] SegMetrics order-bump playbook treats a take rate under 15% as a trigger to test product relevance, and a high take rate with low AOV as a sign of underpricing. — [SegMetrics playbook PDF](https://segmetrics.io/wp-content/uploads/Playbook-Sales-Funnel-Order-Bump.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER, SNIPPET] A course-creator guide sets a bump take-rate target of 10-30% and suggests pricing bumps at roughly 10-20% of the main offer. — [Course Coach: Order Bumps](https://course-coach.pages.dev/courses/create-product-suite/09-order-bumps/)
- [PRACTITIONER, SNIPPET, anecdotal] Online Marketing Classroom: examples of $17 bumps on $47 offers; "the take rate generally sits in the 25 to 40 percent range for most offers I have seen." The same result set contained a "$5 to $15 sweet spot, ideally under 20 percent of the order value" rule; exact page attribution for that sentence was not confirmed. — [Online Marketing Classroom](https://onlinemarketingclassroom.com/blog/?p=1757)
- [VENDOR CLAIM, SNIPPET] Digistore24: well-executed bumps add 20-40% to average order value (an AOV lift, not a take rate). — [Digistore24](https://www.digistore24.com/blog/increase-aov/)
- [UNVERIFIED, SNIPPET] A Tagada glossary cites an unnamed "2023 benchmark study" showing 18-35% AOV gains; no study named. — [Tagada glossary](https://www.tagada.io/glossary/order-bump)
- [VERIFIED] Real price points of low-ticket add-ons beside a $39 vibrating alarm at the direct competitor: $2.95, $7.95, $8.80, $9.95, $18 (none digital content). — [FitSleeps products.json](https://fitsleeps.com/products.json?limit=250)

### Inferences
- The 25-40% figures come from info-product checkouts where the buyer is already buying digital content and the bump is a one-click box on the payment form. A PDF guide next to a physical gadget on a Shopify product page is a different context; the Shopify-specific data (Zipify 4%-16%, 2022: 9.9%) is the more conservative and more relevant anchor.
- Arithmetic for the client's case (my calculation, not a sourced benchmark): at $6.99, take rates of 10% / 20% / 30% add about $0.70 / $1.40 / $2.10 revenue per order, i.e. 1.7% / 3.5% / 5.2% on a $39.99 order. The add-on is nearly all margin but small in absolute terms.
- With 2 orders so far, the store cannot measure a take rate or run an A/B test; any figure above is a planning assumption only. Dozens to hundreds of orders are needed before a take rate means anything.
- Sources warn that take rate is not incremental profit: a bump can displace another add-on or add friction to the main purchase.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found that is specific to (a) digital guides attached to physical products, (b) product-page tick-box placement versus checkout placement, or (c) the sleep category.
- ReConvert, AfterSell, Rebuy, ThriveCart and ClickFunnels figures could not be retrieved (hosts blocked; searches surfaced no specific numbers from them).
- None of the vendor figures state sample period, how "take rate" is defined (per order vs per offer view) or selection effects, as far as the snippets show.

## 3. Paid add-on versus free bonus: which performs better for conversion and for profit at this price point?

### Takeaway
There is no direct test of "paid $7 digital guide vs free guide worth $X" for a ~$40 gadget. The nearest evidence (one vendor-published A/B test in the sleep category, plus academic work on free gifts) suggests a free bonus tends to help conversion slightly while a paid offer can win on profit per visitor; for a store at 2 orders, conversion is the binding constraint and the revenue from a $6.99 bump is small either way.

### Cited Findings
- [VENDOR CASE STUDY, SNIPPET] Intelligems reports a checkout test by Slumber Cloud (bedding): a free Pillow Cover gift vs a discounted Body Pillow upsell. The free gift converted slightly better (2.13% vs 2.01%), but the discounted paid upsell generated about 19% more gross profit per visitor, "with 91% probability to be the better option." Sample size, duration and margins are not given. — [Intelligems: what should I offer at checkout](https://www.intelligems.io/resources/blog/what-should-i-offer-at-checkout-to-improve-profit)
- [VENDOR GUIDANCE, SNIPPET] Intelligems advises judging offers on profit per visitor = (revenue − product costs − gift costs) ÷ visitors, and choosing gifts with high perceived value relative to cost ("A $5 item that feels worth $20-25 is ideal"). — [Intelligems: should I offer gift with purchase](https://www.intelligems.io/resources/blog/should-i-offer-gift-with-purchase)
- [ACADEMIC, via press coverage, 2013] Palmeira & Srivastava (Journal of Consumer Research): people offered an item free valued it more than people offered it at a deep discount (willingness to pay $2.95 vs $1.83 in the spaghetti study); the authors argue buyers use the focal product's price to judge a free bonus, so "free" need not devalue it when paired with a higher-priced purchase. — [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2013-08-consumers-freebie-valuable-discounted-product.html)
- [ACADEMIC, SNIPPET, 2005] Raghubir (Journal of Product & Brand Management): when a product is given away "free", consumers are willing to pay less for it as a stand-alone product, especially when the promotion does not state the price of the free gift. — [Emerald](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/10610420510592617/full/html)
- [ACADEMIC, via trade summary] A Berkeley study reported by MarketingProfs found a free gift with purchase can make consumers denigrate the perceived value of the gifted product, with spillover to other brands in the gift's category. Authors not named in the snippet. — [MarketingProfs](https://marketingprofs.com/short-articles/247/partner-your-product-with-care)
- [AGENCY OPINION, SNIPPET] Scandiweb (CRO agency) says post-purchase offers are accepted at higher rates than product-page or cart upsells, with less risk to the original purchase; not a controlled test. — [Scandiweb](https://scandiweb.com/blog/upselling-techniques-and-examples/)
- [REGULATORY, SNIPPET] FTC Guide Concerning Use of the Word "Free" (16 CFR Part 251): all terms and conditions for receiving the "free" item must be stated clearly and conspicuously at the outset; except for introductory offers, a "free" offer is improper if no substantial sales were made at the stated "regular" price. — [eCFR 16 CFR 251.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-251/section-251.1); [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/guide-concerning-use-word-free-similar-representations)
- [VERIFIED] In the market, the free route is the observed norm: FitSleeps prices its two breathwork items at $0 with no stated value; Manta states a value ("FREE $18") for its napping guide beside a $159 bundle. — [FitSleeps products.json](https://fitsleeps.com/products.json?limit=250); [Manta Sound Calm Bundle](https://mantasleep.com/pages/sound-calm-bundle)

### Inferences
- Stating a believable price for a free guide (as Manta does) is consistent with Raghubir's finding that unpriced free gifts get devalued. A practical way to make a "worth $6.99" claim honest is to genuinely list and sell the guide at $6.99 as a standalone product; a "value" figure with no real sales behind it is the kind of claim the FTC pricing/"free" guides are aimed at. This is not legal advice, and the Part 233 "value/comparable price" text was not read in this session.
- A hybrid seen in the wild is possible on Shopify: sell the guide at $6.99 as a tick-box, and make it free above a threshold or with a 2-pack (couples), which also serves the "couples" audience and mirrors FitSleeps' bundle-gift mechanic and the supplement funnels' "bonuses with bigger packs" mechanic.
- For this store specifically: at 2 orders, the difference between the two options cannot be measured. A paid tick-box risks nothing in gift cost but adds a decision on the product page; a free bonus costs nothing to fulfil and may lift conversion, which is worth far more than $1-2 per order at this stage. The evidence base for either choice is thin and indirect.

### Gaps
- No A/B test found comparing a paid digital add-on with the same item as a free bonus, in any category.
- No data on whether a product-page add-on checkbox reduces main-product conversion for low-traffic stores.
- The Slumber Cloud test compared two physical items at a much higher order value; transfer to a $6.99 PDF is an assumption.
- FTC 16 CFR Part 233 (deceptive pricing / value claims) was not retrieved; confirm wording before making any "worth $X" claim.

## 4. What content topics and formats work for a sleep/wake-up audience, and what length/format do buyers expect for $5-10?

### Takeaway
Observed formats in this niche are short guided audio (FitSleeps: one wake-up breathwork track, one fall-asleep track), a single-topic how-to guide (Manta: napping), tip-list ebooks (Dubs Labs: 52 tips) and in-device audio libraries; nothing found shows which format sells best. Practitioner guidance for low-ticket digital items is to keep them short, specific and quick to use, so a brief, device-specific plan plus one or two audio tracks fits both the evidence and the price.

### Cited Findings
- [VERIFIED] FitSleeps pairs its wristband with two guided-breathwork items split by moment of use: "Calm Start – ... Wake Up Clear & Calm" (morning) and "Night Reset – ... Fall Asleep Faster" (night). — [FitSleeps products.json](https://fitsleeps.com/products.json?limit=250)
- [VERIFIED] Manta's bonus is a single-topic "Napping Guide" pitched on a productivity outcome ("master a well-timed snooze for better productivity"), valued at $18. — [Manta Sound Calm Bundle](https://mantasleep.com/pages/sound-calm-bundle)
- [SNIPPET] Dubs Labs' free ebook is a list format: "Master Your Sleep: 52 Essential Sleep Tips", with a "boost your sleep quality in just 7 days" page headline. — [Dubs Labs](https://dubslabs.com/pages/sleep-tips)
- [SNIPPET] Device brands lean on audio: Loftie lists breathwork exercises, sound baths and guided meditations; Hatch+ ($4.99/month) is a library of audio and light content. — [Loftie wellness content](https://learn.byloftie.com/loftie-clock-wellness-content); [Tom's Guide on Hatch](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/sleep/what-is-the-hatch-restore-3-and-should-you-buy-it)
- [SNIPPET] Supplement funnels use ebooks with lifestyle angles (e.g. evening snacks that disrupt sleep; a sleep-hygiene guide). — [Yu Sleep page](https://yusleepsupplements.com/); [Sleeply review](https://www.sportandmedicalsciences.org/sleeply-reviews/)
- [VENDOR GUIDANCE, SNIPPET] Ebook pricing guides from SamCart and ConvertKit (Kit): B2C ebooks at $7-$27 "tend to convert best"; short-form content (quick guides, checklists) is usually $2.99-$5.99; length should match the promise and value matters more than page count (a 150-page ebook undercuts a "quick" promise). Which of the two pages each sentence came from was not confirmed. — [SamCart: how to price an ebook](https://www.samcart.com/blog/how-to-price-an-ebook); [ConvertKit: ebook pricing](https://convertkit.com/ebook-pricing)
- [VENDOR GUIDANCE, SNIPPET] Common bump formats are companions that help the buyer use the main purchase: a template, workbook, checklist or printable. — [Teachable: how to price an order bump](https://teachable.com/blog/how-to-price-an-order-bump)
- [VERIFIED] AASM survey release: 48% of US adults have used a sleep tracker (up from 35% in 2023), 55% change behaviour based on it, and 76% say they have lost sleep worrying about sleep problems ("orthosomnia"). — [AASM survey release](https://aasm.org/sleep-tracking-and-sleepmaxxing-change-bedtime-behaviors-and-keep-some-americans-awake-at-night/)
- [VERIFIED] FitSleeps' own audience description for the wristband names the same segments as the client: partners/housemates who should not be woken, deep sleepers, chronic snoozers, people who want the phone out of the bedroom, and people with hearing problems. — [FitSleeps products.json](https://fitsleeps.com/products.json?limit=250)

### Inferences
- A credible $6.99 item for this buyer is one that makes the wristband work better, not a generic sleep ebook: e.g. a short "first 7 mornings" set-up and wake-up plan (where to wear it, alarm placement and backup for heavy sleepers, a fixed wake time), a one-page couples' section (two different wake times without waking the partner), a one-page shift-worker section, and one or two short guided audio tracks (wake-up and wind-down). This mirrors the FitSleeps morning/night split and the Manta single-problem guide. This is my synthesis; no source tested these topics.
- Given the price guidance ($2.99-$5.99 for short checklists, $7+ for fuller guides), $6.99 for a PDF alone is at the top of the "quick guide" band; adding audio or printable trackers helps justify it. A short, skimmable PDF (roughly 10-20 pages) with checklists is in line with "length should match the promise"; no source gives a page-count norm.
- Credibility markers buyers can check: named sources (AASM, NIOSH/CDC), a "not medical advice" line, no cure claims, a clear description of what is inside and how it is delivered (FitSleeps gives none), and instant delivery by email.
- The AASM "orthosomnia" finding argues for a calm, low-pressure tone (habits, not scores or fear).

### Gaps
- No sales or take-rate data by topic or format (PDF vs audio vs course) for sleep audiences.
- No sourced norm for page count or audio length at $5-10; standalone sleep guides exist on Gumroad/Payhip but their prices and lengths were not captured.
- No evidence on what US couples, shift workers or heavy sleepers specifically want from a guide (no survey or review mining found).
- Length/format of the FitSleeps tracks and the Manta guide is not disclosed on the pages read.

## 5. What sleep-science content can be stated safely and accurately in such a guide?

### Takeaway
General sleep-habit guidance is well supported by AASM and NIOSH/CDC material and can be used as educational content with attribution: fixed wake time, 7+ hours, light as the main timing cue, caffeine timing, limiting evening light and screens, and basic night-shift tactics. Sleep-inertia duration and especially snoozing are less settled (snoozing studies conflict), and anything about diagnosing or treating insomnia, sleep apnea, shift work disorder, or about melatonin or medication, is medical territory and should be left out or handled with "talk to a health care provider".

### Cited Findings

**Consistent wake time and sleep duration**
- [VERIFIED] AASM "Healthy Sleep Habits" handout: "Get up at the same time every day, even on weekends and vacations"; "Choose a bedtime that allows for at least 7-8 hours of sleep"; "Avoid going to bed before you feel sleepy"; if not asleep "after about 20 minutes, do a quiet activity in low light and avoid electronics." — [AASM Healthy Sleep Habits PDF](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthy-sleep-habits-AASM.pdf)
- [VERIFIED] AASM refers to "the recommended seven or more hours of sleep each night" for adults. — [AASM survey release](https://aasm.org/sleep-tracking-and-sleepmaxxing-change-bedtime-behaviors-and-keep-some-americans-awake-at-night/)
- [VERIFIED] AASM president (2024 eclipse statement): "we sleep best when we maintain a consistent, daily rhythm by waking up and going to sleep at the same time every day"; erratic wake times, time-zone travel and heavy caffeine are named as disruptors. — [AASM on light](https://aasm.org/embracing-the-eclipse-shedding-light-on-the-importance-of-light-for-our-sleep-and-daily-routines/)

**Light**
- [VERIFIED] AASM: "Light and darkness are the most powerful timing cues for alertness and sleepiness in the body"; light regulates hormones such as melatonin according to circadian rhythm. — [AASM on light](https://aasm.org/embracing-the-eclipse-shedding-light-on-the-importance-of-light-for-our-sleep-and-daily-routines/)
- [VERIFIED] AASM handout: "Reduce exposure to bright light before bed"; "Stop using electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime." — [AASM Healthy Sleep Habits PDF](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthy-sleep-habits-AASM.pdf)
- [SNIPPET] Sleep Foundation: getting natural light early in the day can help normalise circadian rhythm. (I did not find a morning-light instruction in the AASM handout itself.) — [Sleep Foundation healthy sleep tips](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/articles/healthy-sleep-tips)

**Caffeine**
- [VERIFIED] AASM handout: "Avoid Afternoon & Evening Caffeine — Limit caffeine later in the day." No specific clock time is given. — [AASM Healthy Sleep Habits PDF](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthy-sleep-habits-AASM.pdf)
- [VERIFIED, 2013] AASM press release on Drake et al. (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine): 400 mg caffeine (about 2-3 cups of coffee) taken 0, 3 or 6 hours before bedtime significantly disrupted sleep; at 6 hours, objectively measured total sleep time fell by more than one hour, and participants did not notice it. Small study: 12 healthy normal sleepers. The authors suggest avoiding caffeine after 5 p.m. — [AASM caffeine release](https://aasm.org/late-afternoon-and-early-evening-caffeine-can-disrupt-sleep-at-night/)

**Other habits on the AASM list (safe to restate with attribution)**
- [VERIFIED] Relaxing bedtime routine; bed only for sleep and sex; bedroom quiet, relaxing and cool; avoid large meals before bed; exercise regularly; "Alcohol can interfere with healthy sleep"; drink less close to bedtime. The handout also says: "If sleep difficulties persist despite practicing healthy sleep habits, talk with your health care provider or seek assistance from an AASM-accredited sleep center." — [AASM Healthy Sleep Habits PDF](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/healthy-sleep-habits-AASM.pdf)

**Sleep inertia (morning grogginess)**
- [SNIPPET] NIOSH training for nurses: sleep inertia "typically can last from 30 to 60 minutes" and has been observed lasting 2 hours; it cites Tassi & Muzet that it usually does not last longer than 30 minutes but can be longer if the person is sleep deprived. Bright light and washing the face have helped restore alertness more quickly after a nap; 100 mg of caffeine on awakening reduced the time of sleep inertia versus placebo. — [NIOSH nurse training, module 7 (archived)](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod7/03.html)
- [SNIPPET] Sleep Foundation: sleep inertia usually lasts 15-60 minutes but may last up to a few hours; keep naps to 30 minutes or less to limit it. — [Sleep Foundation: sleep inertia](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/sleep-inertia)

**Snoozing — evidence is mixed**
- [SNIPPET, 2023] Sundelin et al., Journal of Sleep Research: survey of 1,732 adults found snoozing widespread, especially in younger people and later chronotypes; lab study of 31 habitual snoozers found 30 minutes of snoozing cost about 6 minutes of sleep, prevented awakenings from deep (N3) sleep, and improved or did not affect cognitive performance right after waking; no clear effects on cortisol awakening response, morning sleepiness, mood or overnight sleep architecture. Small sample of habitual snoozers only. — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/health/sleep/hitting-snooze-can-boost-morning-cognition-study-hints); [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/is-snoozing-the-alarm-good-or-bad-for-your-health)
- [SNIPPET, 2022] Mattingly et al., SLEEP 45(10), DOI 10.1093/sleep/zsac184: among 450 US adults in full-time salaried jobs, 57% were habitual snoozers; the lead author cautioned the sample is not representative of the general population. Snoozers showed lighter sleep in the final hour before waking. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/968205)
- [SNIPPET] Sleep-inertia researcher Cassie Hilditch (not involved in the 2023 study) said the snoozing-cognition relationship needs further research and noted other work reporting increased sleep inertia and worse performance in snoozers. — [Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/is-snoozing-the-alarm-good-or-bad-for-your-health)

**Shift workers**
- [SNIPPET] NIOSH: night workers usually get less and poorer-quality sleep than day workers; suggested tactics include a long nap before a night shift, not staying awake all day before the first night shift, going directly to bed on arriving home, and minimising bright light in the second half of the shift; workers with ongoing sleep problems should see a health care provider. A "caffeine in the first half of the shift only" tip was seen only second-hand. — [NIOSH: Shift Work and Sleep (2016)](https://cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2016/shift-work.html); [NIOSH nurse training, module 9](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod9/05.html)
- [VERIFIED] AASM clinical guideline release on shift work disorder: the disorder affects approximately 27% of adult shift workers; "There is no one-size-fits-all solution"; the guideline suggests considering caffeine, bright light plus caffeine, strategic naps plus caffeine, scheduled bright light exposure, meal timing, "consistent sleep scheduling when feasible" and "light-filtering strategies during the commute home", while noting low certainty of evidence for these. The same guideline covers armodafinil/modafinil, melatonin, hypnotics and CBT-I, which are clinical treatments. (Publication date not captured.) — [AASM shift work disorder guideline release](https://aasm.org/new-guideline-provides-recommendations-on-managing-shift-work-disorder/)

**Competitor claims that illustrate the line not to cross**
- [VERIFIED] FitSleeps product copy includes health-outcome claims such as mouth tape that can "reduce snoring", nasal strips "improving airflow by up to 40%", and glasses giving "faster sleep onset, more REM sleep". — [FitSleeps products.json](https://fitsleeps.com/products.json?limit=250)

### Inferences
- Safe to state as general education with attribution: keep one wake time seven days a week; plan bedtime for 7+ hours; light and darkness are the main timing cues, so dim light and put screens away before bed and get light after waking; limit caffeine later in the day (optionally citing the small 2013 study as "one study found..."); expect grogginess after waking that usually fades within about 30-60 minutes; basic NIOSH shift-work tactics.
- Should be worded cautiously: snoozing. The accurate statement is that research is mixed and limited; a guide should not say snoozing is harmful or beneficial as fact. A defensible practical framing is "set the alarm for the time you actually need to get up" rather than a health claim.
- Medical territory to avoid: claiming the guide or wristband treats, cures or prevents insomnia, sleep apnea, shift work disorder, hypersomnia or "sleep disorders"; dosing advice for melatonin or any drug; promises of specific physiological outcomes (more REM, deeper sleep, X minutes more sleep); and telling heavy sleepers their difficulty waking is normal. Persistent trouble sleeping or waking warrants the AASM-style line to talk with a health care provider.
- The breathwork angle (as FitSleeps uses) should be framed as relaxation, not as a proven way to "fall asleep faster"; I found no authoritative source in this session supporting a specific breathwork-sleep claim.
- Attributing statements ("AASM recommends...") is accurate; implying AASM, CDC or NIOSH endorse the product or guide would not be.
