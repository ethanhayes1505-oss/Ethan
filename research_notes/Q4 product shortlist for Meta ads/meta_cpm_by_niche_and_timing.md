## 1. Typical US Meta CPM, CTR, CPC, conversion rate and cost per purchase by ecommerce category (2026)

### Takeaway
The best ecommerce-specific dataset found (Triple Whale, Aug 2025–Jul 2026, reported as 40,000+ brands) puts the median Meta CPM at about $15 and median cost per purchase at about $39, with category CPMs running from about $11.50 (baby) to about $22 (health & wellness) and category cost per purchase from about $30 (baby) to about $51 (electronics). Health & wellness is consistently the dearest category to reach; no source found gives figures for jewellery, gifts, hobbies, automotive accessories or seniors/mobility aids.

### Inferences
- Ranking of the categories for which numbers exist, cheapest to dearest to *reach*: baby (~$11.50) < apparel/accessories (~$13–14) < all-ecommerce median (~$15; pets was ~$15.50 in 2024) < beauty (~$19.50) < health & wellness (~$21–22). Health & wellness CPM is roughly 45% above the ecommerce median and about 90% above baby (21.80 / 15.06 = 1.45; 21.80 / 11.50 = 1.90).
- Ranking by cost per purchase is different: baby ($29.87) and lifestyle/boutique ($32.04) are lowest; home & garden ($48.61) and electronics ($51.06) are highest even though they are not the dearest to reach. That probably reflects higher order values and longer consideration, not auction price — so "cheap CPM" and "cheap purchase" are separate questions.
- Health & wellness pairs the highest CPM with an above-median CTR (3.02% vs 2.39%) and a mid-table CPA ($42.30): expensive impressions partly offset by strong response.
- These medians come from established brands whose average order value is usually well above $40 (order values were not captured — see Gaps). A median cost per purchase of ~$39 against a $39.99 single-item order means the *median* brand at this price point would roughly break even on ad cost alone before product cost. A $40 one-off gadget has to beat the category median CPA substantially, not just match it.
- Because US & Canada price per ad was up 23% year on year in Q3 2026, 2025 seasonal dollar figures should be scaled up when used to plan Q4 2026/January 2027 (a straight carry-forward of +23% is an assumption, not a measurement).
- Datasets differ by a factor of two or more in level (Gupta all-vertical ~$7–10 vs Triple Whale ecommerce ~$15) because of advertiser mix and objective mix. Compare categories within one dataset only.

### Gaps
- No category figures found for jewellery, electronics *accessories*, gifts, hobbies, automotive accessories, or seniors/mobility aids. Triple Whale's 15-industry table has no jewellery row; nobody publishes a "seniors/mobility" vertical.
- Could not open Triple Whale, Lebesgue (industry × AOV benchmarks), Varos (wellness page), Northbeam, Motion, Revealbot/Birch, Madgicx, Databox or Statista. Lebesgue's AOV-bucket table would be the most useful missing piece for a $40 product.
- A Databox figure (median Meta CPM $7.26, updated 22 October 2026) and a SuperAds figure (US monthly CPM peaking at $28.09 in November 2025 and ending January 2026 at $17.12; another dataset gave a November 2025 peak of $25.22) appeared in search summaries but I could not pin the originating page URLs, so they are not cited above. If the SuperAds US series is real it implies a November-to-January fall of about 39% (17.12 / 28.09 = 0.61).
- No 2026 full-year category table with sample sizes per category was seen.

## 2. CPM by age, gender and placement in the US — is an older female audience cheaper or dearer, and does it convert?

### Takeaway
There is no reliable published US CPM table by age and gender (Meta does not publish one), and the scraps that exist disagree. The balance of evidence — including this seller's own test — is that people 65+ are often **dearer per impression** but can be **cheaper per purchase**, and that women cost somewhat more to reach than men; Reels and Stories impressions are cheaper than Feed, but Meta's own experiment says letting the system choose placements lowers cost per result.

### Cited Findings
**Gender**
- Lebesgue ("2025 update"): average CPM targeting women $6.01 versus men $4.92 — not split by age or country. UNVERIFIED-A — [Lebesgue CPM by gender](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/facebook-cpm-by-gender)

**Age**
- Noble Growth 2026 account data: CPM for 65+ was $35.14; people 65+ cost "about fourteen times more to reach" than 18–24, while a purchase from them cost "about half as much". Sample size and advertiser mix not seen. UNVERIFIED-B — [Noble Growth benchmarks](https://noblegrowth.co/tools/meta-ads-benchmarks/)
- ROI Magnet (agency, several accounts, **India**, rupees): "CPM rises as age increases"; example account 55–64 at ₹250 versus ₹142 for 25–34. UNVERIFIED-B, not US — [ROI Magnet](https://www.roimagnet.com/post/decoding-cpm-in-meta-ads-why-age-matters)
- AdSpendSpy **modelled** 2026 estimates for the EU/UK (it notes Meta publishes no such data for the US): men 25–34 €7.50, men 55–64 €6.60, men 65+ €6.10 — i.e. older is cheaper in this model. UNVERIFIED-B, modelled, not US — [AdSpendSpy](https://www.adspendspy.com/facebook-cpm-by-country)
- StackMatix: targeting ages 25–44 costs 20–40% more than 45–65+. Unsourced generalisation. UNVERIFIED-B — [StackMatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/facebook-ads-cost-complete-guide)
- Jon Loomer (practitioner): when he optimises for leads, Meta often spends a high share of budget on people over 65. Anecdote, lead objective. UNVERIFIED-B — [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/targeting-2/)

**Placement**
- Influee (citing a "Mintec 2026 DTC comparison"; undated ranges): Facebook Feed $8–$14, Instagram Feed $7–$12, Facebook Stories $5–$9, Instagram Stories $5–$9, Facebook Reels $4–$8; many accounts see Reels 30–50% cheaper than Feed for a comparable audience; "lower CPM on Reels does not guarantee lower CPA"; Reels discount described as narrowing. UNVERIFIED-B — [Influee](https://influee.co/blog/good-cpm-facebook-ads)
- Meta: ad sets using Advantage+ placements delivered an 11.7% lower cost per action on average than manual placements, "based on budget-controlled experiments on 147k campaigns that ran from August 30 to September 7, 2025". VERIFIED-META — [About Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/196554084569964)
- Meta: the sales, leads and app-promotion objectives "now default to Advantage+ placements and the option to make placement selections is removed in ad sets" (gradual rollout); placement exclusions remain possible at account level. VERIFIED-META — [About Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/196554084569964)
- Meta: with the highest-volume bid strategy the system seeks the lowest average cost overall, "not the lowest average cost for each placement"; "one placement's average cost per optimization event being higher than another's doesn't necessarily mean it's inefficient" (worked example: removing the dearer placement cut results from 9 for $27 to 8 for $26). VERIFIED-META — [How the Meta delivery system works using Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/965529646866485)
- Meta recommends staying opted into all placements across Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Threads and Audience Network. VERIFIED-META — [Best practices for placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/23942209872116948)

### Inferences
- This seller's test (CPM about £49 ≈ $63, both buyers women 65+) fits the Noble Growth pattern (65+ CPM ~$35, expensive to reach, cheaper per purchase) better than the "older is cheaper" claims. With purchase optimisation the system concentrates on whoever it predicts will buy; if those are women 65+, the campaign pays what that audience costs.
- "Older women are a cheap audience" is not supported by any US dataset found. The defensible statement is: older audiences are not cheap to *reach* under purchase optimisation, but may be efficient per *purchase* for products that genuinely suit them.
- Women ~22% dearer than men on Lebesgue's numbers (6.01 / 4.92 = 1.22) — plausible because more ecommerce advertisers compete for women, but the figure is global and old.
- Placement mix moves CPM a lot (Reels at roughly half of Feed in the quoted ranges), so a campaign whose creative only works in Facebook Feed — typical for a 65+ audience, who are mostly on Facebook rather than Instagram Reels — will show a higher blended CPM than a vertical-video campaign reaching 25–44s. This is an inference; no source measured it.
- No evidence was found on how well 65+ women convert for inexpensive gadgets specifically. Two purchases is far too few to conclude anything about this seller's audience.

### Gaps
- No US age × gender CPM table from a named dataset with sample size (Varos, Triple Whale and Meta do not publish one publicly). Women 55–65+ versus 25–44 could not be quantified.
- No Audience Network CPM benchmark found.
- A Q3 2026 placement study of 18 local/DTC accounts (connercrowe.com) appeared in results but could not be read.
- The seller's own Ads Manager breakdown by age/gender and placement is the only like-for-like source; it was not pulled in this research task.

## 3. The CPM curve from early November to late January (2023, 2024, 2025 and anything for 2026)

### Takeaway
Across independent trackers the pattern is consistent: CPM climbs through November, peaks on or just after Black Friday/Cyber Monday (roughly +60–85% above a September baseline on the peak days), stays high for the first one to three weeks of December, drops sharply in the week before Christmas, hits its low in the week after Christmas (Gupta: −60% from the 2025 peak week) and stays below every November week throughout January. No measured 2026 Black Friday data exists yet (Black Friday is 27 November 2026, four days after this research).

### Cited Findings
**2025 season (most recent complete year)**
- Gupta Media tracker, weekly Meta CPM (all verticals), as relayed by a secondary blog: week of 3 Nov 2025 $8.15 → peak $12.48 in the week of 1 Dec 2025 → $4.93 in the week of 29 Dec 2025 (−60% from peak); every January 2026 week between $6.59 and $6.99, below every November week. UNVERIFIED-B (secondary relay of a named dataset) — [GAAS, "Meta ads got 60% cheaper the week after Christmas"](https://www.trygaas.com/blog/january-meta-cpm-window)
- Gupta Media: Meta CPMs averaged 40% less from 26 Dec 2025 to 31 Jan 2026 than in the first 24 days of December 2025; Gupta now plans "Q5" as 26 December to 31 January "because prices in our data stayed below December levels through January". UNVERIFIED-A — [Gupta Media Q5 guide](https://www.guptamedia.com/insights/q5-marketing-holiday-advertising)
- Gupta Media: average Meta CPM in December 2025 $10.02. UNVERIFIED-A — [Gupta Media tracker](https://www.guptamedia.com/social-media-ads-cost)
- Lebesgue (large-advertiser sample, median brand, versus September): Black Friday week 2025 CPM about +59%; Black Friday itself about +84%; Cyber Monday +71%; the Tuesday after +48%; the Wednesday +20%. UNVERIFIED-A — [Lebesgue, "Are Meta ads worth it on Black Friday?"](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/are-meta-ads-worth-it-black-friday)
- Run-up shape from one 2026 agency curve post (which of two pages in the result set it came from was not confirmed): CPM about 4% above an October baseline in the week beginning 9 November, 24% above the following week, and 57% above in Black Friday week. UNVERIFIED-B — [Adrise Lab Q4 curve](https://adriselab.com/blog/q4-meta-ads-cpm-curve-2026)
- Category differences in the Black Friday-week rise (2025; attributed in the result set to the Lebesgue/NAMEX analyses, exact page not confirmed): home & decor +116%, fashion & footwear +66%, beauty/health/supplements +42%. Same analysis: Black Friday-week conversion rate +48% and cost per purchase −20% versus September despite higher CPMs. UNVERIFIED-B — [NAMEX Black Friday ad costs](https://www.namediaexperts.com/blog-posts/black-friday-q4-ad-cost-benchmarks-2026-cpm-cpc-by-channel)
- Biddy (claims $35M+ of ad spend, consumer brands): January 2026 CPM about 17% below October 2025; 29 Dec 2025–4 Jan 2026 CPM 24% below October and cost per purchase 24% lower. Its peak-week claim (15–21 December, +35%) contradicts Gupta and Lebesgue and its table was garbled in the summary. UNVERIFIED-B — [Biddy Black Friday CPM](https://www.biddyco.com/blog-posts/meta-ads-black-friday-cpm)
- GeistM (publisher/advertiser network data): in 2025 CPMs were down only 10% by the end of December after an early-December rise. UNVERIFIED-A — [GeistM Q4 2025 rate trends](https://geistm.com/blog/meta-rate-trends-q4-2025/)
- Triple Whale BFCM 2025 (33,000+ shops): Meta CPM about $22.26, +7.6–7.8% year on year. UNVERIFIED-A — [Triple Whale BFCM 2025](https://www.triplewhale.com/bfcm-2025)

**2024 season**
- Gupta Media: Cyber Monday (2 Dec 2024) was the most expensive day at $17.70 CPM; Black Friday (29 Nov 2024) second at $16.85; Black Friday week 2024 averaged $13.42. UNVERIFIED-B (secondary relay; one blog mislabels these as 2025) — [Clouted, citing Gupta Media](https://clouted.com/blog/meta-advertising-CPM-inflation-statistics)
- Lebesgue: Black Friday week 2024 CPM 80% above September for the same brands. UNVERIFIED-A — [Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/are-meta-ads-worth-it-black-friday)
- GeistM: CPMs fell 32% in the second half of December 2024. UNVERIFIED-A — [GeistM Q4 2024 insights](https://geistm.com/q4-meta-advertising-insights/)

**2022–2023 seasons**
- Gupta Media's two-year study (published late 2023): the notable price drop comes in **week 51**; prices stayed high from Black Friday through the following weeks and fell to $7.06 on 18 December; the second-to-last week of the year ran about 20% cheaper than the rest of the Q4 rush. UNVERIFIED-A — [Gupta Media, "Q5: fact or fiction?"](https://guptamedia.substack.com/p/q5-fact-or-fiction)
- Same study, as reported by MediaPost: week-51 CPMs were still 8% *higher* than the Q1–Q3 average and less than 1% cheaper than the Q4 average — i.e. the dip is relative to the holiday peak, not to a normal month. Meta itself defined Q5 as 18 December to 31 January. UNVERIFIED-A — [MediaPost, "What's your plan for Q5?"](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/390818/whats-your-plan-for-q5.html)

**2026**
- No measured Black Friday 2026 data exists yet. Planning rules of thumb published for 2026: budget for Black Friday-week CPMs at 1.6–1.8× your own September average. UNVERIFIED-B — [NAMEX](https://www.namediaexperts.com/blog-posts/black-friday-q4-ad-cost-benchmarks-2026-cpm-cpc-by-channel)
- Vendor seasonal index: November Meta CPMs run 60–100% above January levels. UNVERIFIED-B — [StackMatix seasonal index](https://stackmatix.com/blog/seasonal-ad-cost-fluctuations-2026)
- Meta's Q3 2026 price per ad was up 23% year on year in the US & Canada, so the 2026 curve starts from a higher base than 2025. UNVERIFIED-A — [Meta Q3 2026 results via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/405782/meta-reports-third-quarter-2026-results)

### Inferences
- **Mapped onto the 2026/27 calendar** (my projection from the 2023–2025 pattern, not measured data; Black Friday 27 Nov, Cyber Monday 30 Nov 2026):
  - 9–22 Nov: already +4% to +24% over October and rising. The seller's test ran in this window.
  - 23 Nov–1 Dec (Black Friday week through Cyber Monday and the day after): the peak; roughly +60–85% over September on the peak days.
  - 2–c.14 Dec: off the single-day peak within 2–3 days but still elevated (Lebesgue had +20% over September two days after Cyber Monday; Gupta's weekly series had its highest *week* in the Cyber Monday week and December 2025 averaging $10.02 versus $8.15 in early November).
  - c.14–24 Dec (week 51, after ground-shipping cut-offs): the first real fall — roughly 20% (Gupta 2023) to 32% (GeistM 2024) below the earlier December level, though only 10% in GeistM's 2025 data.
  - 26 Dec–c.4 Jan: the low point — Gupta −60% from the 2025 peak week; Biddy −24% versus October.
  - January: stable and cheap — Gupta weekly $6.59–$6.99 against $8.15 in early November 2025 (about 14–19% below early November); Biddy −17% versus October; Gupta −40% versus 1–24 December.
- Two different comparisons are being mixed in practitioner talk about "Q5". Versus the **holiday peak**, Q5 is 40–60% cheaper. Versus a **normal month** (October), Q5/January is only about 15–25% cheaper. The second number is the honest one for deciding whether January makes a product viable.
- Applying that to this seller: if all else stayed the same, a mid-November CPM of about $63 would fall to perhaps $40–$50 in January (−20% to −35%). Seasonality alone will **not** bring a $63 CPM down to the ~$15–$20 that a $28–$45 break-even needs; most of the gap is structural (see sections 5–7).
- Higher Black Friday CPMs came with better conversion in the large-brand datasets (conversion rate +48%, cost per purchase −20% versus September). That benefit accrues to brands running real discounts to warm audiences; it should not be assumed for a new store with no list and no offer.
- The 2025 peak week in Gupta's series (week of 1 Dec) contained Cyber Monday (1 Dec 2025). In 2026 Cyber Monday is 30 November, so the peak week is the week of 23–30 November.

### Gaps
- No day-by-day Gupta series for BFCM 2025 was readable; the 2024 daily figures ($16.85/$17.70) were seen only via secondary blogs.
- No 2026 Black Friday or December data (does not exist yet). No US-only weekly series from a named ecommerce dataset; Gupta's is all-vertical and its geography was not confirmed.
- 2023-season week-by-week percentages were not found beyond Gupta's two-year study.
- Sources disagree on the size of the late-December fall (−10% to −60% depending on baseline and year) and on the peak week (Biddy's 15–21 December claim is an outlier).
- Tinuiti, Skai, Emplifi, Sensor Tower, Northbeam, Motion, Common Thread Collective and Revealbot/Birch reports could not be opened.

## 4. Which product types do well in "Q5" and January, and why advertisers say it suits small budgets

### Takeaway
The argument for Q5 is well supported on the cost side (cheaper impressions because big retailers stop bidding after shipping cut-offs while people are still scrolling) but poorly evidenced on the product side: I found no dataset showing conversion rates by product type for late December and January. The "new year, new me" product list (fitness, health habits, sleep, organisation, self-improvement) is practitioner consensus, not measured.

### Cited Findings
- Meta defined Q5 as 18 December to 31 January; Gupta Media's current working window is 26 December to 31 January. UNVERIFIED-A — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/390818/whats-your-plan-for-q5.html); [Gupta Media Q5 guide](https://www.guptamedia.com/insights/q5-marketing-holiday-advertising)
- Why prices fall: many advertisers reduce or pause campaigns after shipping deadlines, so auction competition drops from late December into mid/late January. UNVERIFIED-B — [LeadEnforce](https://leadenforce.com/blog/how-to-capitalize-on-lower-cpms-in-early-q1)
- Some media buyers told Marketing Brew that selected clients planned to buy ads in the Q5 period (late December into January) rather than spend during the holidays, because of high Meta prices. UNVERIFIED-A (trade press reporting) — [Marketing Brew](https://marketingbrew.com/stories/media-buyers-navigating-higher-ad-prices-on-meta)
- Attention stays high after Christmas: Gupta reports TikTok views up 24% in the week after Christmas versus the Q4 average (TikTok, not Meta). UNVERIFIED-A — [Gupta Media Q5 guide](https://www.guptamedia.com/insights/q5-marketing-holiday-advertising)
- Biddy: 29 Dec 2025–4 Jan 2026 cost per purchase 24% below October for the median consumer brand, alongside CPM 24% below October. UNVERIFIED-B — [Biddy](https://www.biddyco.com/blog-posts/meta-ads-black-friday-cpm)
- Counter-evidence on fitness specifically: one fitness/wellness guide says January conversion rates rise roughly 40–60% on New Year's resolutions **but CPMs rise 20–30%** because of resolution campaigns. UNVERIFIED-B — [Benly fitness & wellness playbook](https://benly.ai/learn/meta-ads/meta-ads-fitness-wellness-brands)
- MediaPost's caution on the original Gupta study: even after the dip, CPMs "may still be more expensive than other non-holiday weeks"; the case for Q5 rests on getting more conversions per impression, not just cheaper impressions. UNVERIFIED-A — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/390818/whats-your-plan-for-q5.html)

### Inferences
- Why Q5 suits small budgets (reasoned from the above): (a) the same daily budget buys more impressions when prices are 15–25% below a normal month and 40–60% below the peak; (b) products bought for oneself are not constrained by Christmas delivery dates; (c) gift-card money and returns are being spent; (d) fewer large advertisers are in the auction, so a new account is outbid less often.
- The obvious January categories (fitness, weight, supplements) are also the ones where competition *rises* in January (the 20–30% CPM uplift claim) and where Meta's health policies bite hardest. Less contested self-purchase categories — home organisation, sleep/bedroom, hobbies, habit and routine products, pets — get the cheap auction without the resolution-season bidding war. This is a logical inference; no dataset confirms it.
- A sleep/wake product (such as a vibrating alarm) fits the January "fix my routine" theme, but it sits on the edge of the health category (see section 6), and a 65+ buyer base is unlikely to be driven by resolution season.

### Gaps
- No dataset found on January/Q5 conversion rate or cost per purchase by product category (self-improvement, sleep, organisation, hobbies, home).
- Meta's own Q5 guidance for advertisers was not retrieved.
- No evidence on whether new ad accounts specifically do better in Q5 than in other months.

## 5. Why a small new account can see CPMs of £40–£50 ($50–$65) when optimising for purchases, and what lowers CPM

### Takeaway
Meta's own documentation explains the mechanism: under purchase optimisation the system's bid per impression is "bid per estimated action × estimated action rate", so it deliberately pays a lot for the few people it predicts will buy, and a tiny budget that cannot produce about 50 purchases a week leaves the ad set "learning limited". Meta documents that ad relevance (estimated action rates plus ad quality), a larger audience, more placements and a more frequent optimisation event reduce cost; claims about "account trust", "warming up the pixel", video always beating static, and specific CTR thresholds are folk wisdom with no Meta documentation behind them.

### Cited Findings
**Supported by Meta's own documentation (all VERIFIED-META)**
- The auction winner is the ad with the highest *total value*, a combination of (1) the advertiser's bid, (2) estimated action rates — the probability that showing this ad to this person leads to the advertiser's desired outcome — and (3) ad quality (feedback from people viewing or hiding the ad, and low-quality attributes such as withholding information, sensationalised language and engagement bait). "An ad that's more relevant to a person could win an auction against ads with higher bids." — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- "Bid per impression = bid per estimated action × estimated action rate." — [About bid cap](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/272503946776144)
- Everyone whose target audience includes a person competes for that person; a person "can fall into multiple target audiences". — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- Meta "may use data about you and your ad account to make adjustments that could affect auction outcomes and prices", for example to improve ad quality and relevance or to enforce its policies; there is also a price floor. — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- "Ads that are more relevant cost less and see more results." The diagnostics (quality ranking, engagement rate ranking, conversion rate ranking) are not themselves inputs to the auction, and are not shown for ads with fewer than 500 impressions. — [About ad relevance diagnostics](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/403110480493160)
- Engagement rate ranking is based on the expected likelihood that a person will "click, react to, comment on, share or expand an ad"; engagement bait does not help. — [About engagement rate ranking](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/2351270371824148)
- "It's more impactful to move a ranking from low to average than … from average to above average." Remedies listed: better creative, better post-click experience (landing page), better creative/audience fit, or "target broadly to rely on our delivery system". "Some products and services naturally exhibit lower conversion rates than others." — [How to use ad relevance diagnostics](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/436113280262012)
- An ad set is "learning limited" when it is unlikely to get about 50 optimisation events in the week after the last significant edit. Causes named: small audience size, low budget, low bid or cost control, high auction overlap, an infrequent optimisation event, too many ads at once. Fixes named: combine ad sets, expand the audience, raise the budget, raise the bid/cost control, or "change your optimization event … for example, move from purchases to add to cart". "Learning limited isn't a penalty." — [About learning limited](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/269269737396981)
- Costs should be judged after about 50 optimisation events, when delivery is stable. — [Cost per optimization event](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1707952432550214)
- Advantage+ placements gave 11.7% lower cost per action than manual placements in budget-controlled experiments on 147k campaigns, 30 Aug–7 Sep 2025. — [About Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/196554084569964)

**Practitioner explanations (not Meta documentation)**
- A purchase-optimised campaign looks for different, scarcer people than a click-optimised one, "often" at different effective CPMs; it can cost more per impression or click and still cost less per purchase. UNVERIFIED-B — [Serafinity](https://serafinity.com/blog/why-is-my-cpm-so-high-meta-ads)
- On a small budget the purchase event is too rare to teach the algorithm before the ad set stalls; small accounts often start on a mid-funnel event and move to purchase once volume supports it. UNVERIFIED-B — [AdLibrary small-budget guide](https://adlibrary.com/posts/meta-ads-low-budget-strategy)
- Splitting a small budget over many ad sets is a common cause of higher CPMs; one Shopify merchant reported CPM stepping up after launching several small-budget ad sets, with each restructure restarting learning. UNVERIFIED-B (single anecdote) — [Shopify Community thread](https://community.shopify.com/t/seeking-advice-meta-ads-performance-issues/407187)

### Inferences
- **Why $50–$65 CPM is not surprising for this account** (assembled from the documented mechanics): (1) purchase optimisation multiplies a high value per purchase by the predicted purchase rate, so the system bids aggressively on a small pool of predicted buyers; (2) with ~2 purchases and a new pixel the predictions are poor, so it falls back on expensive, heavily contested proven shoppers; (3) the people it found — women 65+ — appear to be an expensive audience (section 2); (4) mid-to-late November is +4–24% above October and rising (section 3); (5) a product near the health category competes with the dearest ecommerce vertical (section 1).
- **Budget arithmetic against Meta's 50-events guideline:** $45/day = $315/week. Fifty purchases a week at a $40 cost per purchase needs $2,000/week (about $286/day). With $315/week, 50 events requires a cost per event of $6.30 or less (315 / 50) — which is add-to-cart or view-content territory, not purchases. Meta itself suggests moving from purchases to add to cart in this situation. At $45/day a purchase-optimised ad set will be learning limited indefinitely; that is expected, not a fault.
- **What is documented to lower cost:** better relevance (creative that gets more clicks/engagement and fewer hides — raises estimated action rate and quality); broader audience; all placements; one consolidated ad set; a more frequent optimisation event; a better landing page (via conversion rate, which feeds estimated action rate for purchase optimisation).
- **What is folk wisdom (no Meta documentation found in this session):** "new accounts are penalised until they build trust" (Meta says only that account data *may* be used for adjustments — it does not say new accounts pay more); "warming up the pixel"; "video always gets cheaper CPM than static"; specific CTR thresholds that unlock cheaper CPM; turning off Audience Network to cut cost (Meta's own example shows removing a placement can raise overall cost per result).
- A higher CTR lowers CPM only indirectly and partially: it raises the estimated action rate, which lets the same bid win more auctions. It does not change who else is bidding for women 65+ in late November.
- CPM is the wrong target. Under purchase optimisation a high CPM with a high conversion rate can be fine; the number to manage is cost per purchase. For this seller the test produced a cost per purchase of about £112 ($144), far above break-even, so the high CPM was not being repaid by conversion.

### Gaps
- No measured comparison of CPM under purchase optimisation versus link-click optimisation was found (Revealbot/Birch's cost-by-objective data could not be opened).
- No Meta statement was found that new ad accounts pay higher CPMs, nor evidence that they do not.
- No credible dataset on video versus static CPM.
- Jon Loomer and Foxwell Digital material on these specific points was not retrieved.

## 6. What "large market" means for CPM; are niche health products dearer; Meta policy restrictions on health/sleep/pain products

### Takeaway
CPM is set by how many advertisers want the same *people* and how much each expected action is worth to them, not by the size of the product's market; a broad product gives the system more cheap people to choose from, while a narrow health product confines it to a smaller, hotly contested pool — and health & wellness is the dearest ecommerce category on Meta. Health-adjacent stores also face two documented policy risks: Meta may classify the website as a "health and wellness" data source and restrict or block purchase events, and ad copy may not imply the viewer has a health condition or use sensational health claims.

### Cited Findings
**Auction mechanics (VERIFIED-META)**
- Advertisers define audiences; a person can fall into many advertisers' audiences, and all eligible ads compete for each impression. Winner = highest total value (bid × estimated action rate, plus quality). — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- Small audience size and high auction overlap are named causes of "learning limited"; "the larger the audience, the more opportunities for people to complete your optimization event". — [About learning limited](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/269269737396981)

**Category price evidence**
- Health & Wellness has the highest median CPM of the Triple Whale categories quoted ($21.80 vs $15.06 overall; $20.70 in 2025, up 38.03% on the prior year per Influee). UNVERIFIED-A/B — [Triple Whale benchmarks](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks); [Influee](https://influee.co/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks)
- Agency rule of thumb: beauty, supplements and finance regularly $25–$40+ CPM versus $8–$20 for most ecommerce. UNVERIFIED-B — [Top Growth Marketing](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/what-is-good-cpm-for-facebook-and-instagram-ads/)
- eMarketer headline (article behind login): "Meta ad prices swing widely depending on the industry". UNVERIFIED-A (headline only) — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/meta-ad-prices-swing-widely-depending-on-industry)

**Data restrictions for health and wellness websites (VERIFIED-META)**
- Meta categorises data sources (website domains, apps) that send it events. "Health and wellness" is defined as a source "associated with medical conditions or specific health statuses, provider/patient relationships, services for accessing personal health information, or **health-related products and services**" (examples given: patient portal, wellness tracker, telemedicine platform, pharmacy, health insurance marketplace, condition-specific support group). — [About data source categories in Meta Events Manager](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1402913027039332)
- Three restriction levels: **core setup** (blocks custom parameters and the parts of URLs after the domain); **restriction on certain standard events** ("restricts the sharing of specific mid and lower funnel events"); **full restrictions** (no events at all; Meta business tools "cannot be used for ads purposes"). Under event restrictions Meta suggests "leveraging more upper-funnel events"; under full restrictions, optimising for awareness, engagement or traffic. — [Understand data sharing restrictions based on data source categories](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/511197658391698)
- Custom events are automatically blocked on restricted data sources until reviewed, and may be unavailable under full restrictions. — [Understand data sharing restrictions based on data source categories](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/511197658391698)
- Advertisers may be asked to self-categorise within 60 days; Meta can also assign a category, which the advertiser cannot change but can ask to have reviewed in Events Manager (Datasets → Settings → Manage data source categories). Meta says it "will inform you via email and notifications in Meta Events Manager" if it limits or restricts a source. — [How to manage data source categories](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/467621355878794); [Understand data sharing restrictions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/511197658391698)

**How the restrictions are described by third parties**
- Under the mid tier, properties lose Purchase and Lead optimisation and are pushed to upper-funnel events such as PageView and ViewContent; under the full tier, conversion tracking is lost. UNVERIFIED-B — [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/meta-ads-restrictions-health-wellness-campaigns-453094)
- Law-firm reading (January 2025): products "intended to support general wellness (e.g., certain vitamins)" or used for cosmetic purposes "may be less likely" to fall in the health and wellness category. UNVERIFIED-B (legal commentary) — [Foley Hoag](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/security-privacy-and-the-law/2025/january/meta-s-new-advertising-rules-key-considerations-for-health-and-wellness-businesses/)
- Workarounds are disputed: a Shopify forum post says Conversions API/offline events do not get purchase events accepted and suggests a generically named custom event; Freshpaint says custom events cannot mirror a standard event and are unavailable under full restrictions. UNVERIFIED-B — [Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/meta-pixel-restriction-on-health-and-wellness-category/394715); [Freshpaint](https://freshpaint.io/blog/meta-data-restrictions)
- One vendor claims Meta does not notify advertisers when they are placed in a tier — this **contradicts** Meta's own help article quoted above. UNVERIFIED-B — [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/meta-ads-restrictions-health-wellness-campaigns-453094); contradicted by [Meta](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/511197658391698)

**Ad content rules (VERIFIED-META)**
- Meta does not allow ads that "imply or attempt to generate negative self-perception in order to promote diet, weight loss, or other health related products". Not allowed: claims to cure, heal or eliminate a listed set of diseases (diabetes, herpes, thyroid, psoriasis, ebola, cancer, autism, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS, HIV) — symptom-management claims are exempt; "clickbait tactics in a health … context, such as sensational language with exaggerated or extreme claims, or promises of specific outcomes within a set timeframe without disclaimers". — [About Meta's health and wellness advertising policy](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/2489235377779939)
- Separate "personal attributes" policy: ads must avoid "assumptions about someone's personal attributes"; a "personal health and appearance" policy prohibits ads that generate negative self-perception or imply unrealistic results. (Only the one-line summaries were retrieved; the full personal-attributes text was not.) — [About Meta advertising standards](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/488043719226449)

### Inferences
- **"Large market" in CPM terms** means: many people for whom the ad is relevant, spread across many cheap inventory pockets, so the system can skip the expensive ones. A general household, pet, gift or hobby product lets the system pick from tens of millions of people. A product for a specific health situation restricts it to a small group that pharma, hearing-aid, supplement, insurance and medical-device advertisers — with far higher customer values — also want. Their bids set the price.
- Niche health products are dearer on both counts: the category's median CPM is the highest (~$22 versus ~$15), and the narrow audience reduces the system's room to find cheap impressions.
- **Policy risk adds an expected cost** on top of the price: if Meta classifies a health-adjacent store as health and wellness with event restrictions, purchase optimisation stops working and the seller is pushed to upper-funnel optimisation, which normally means worse cost per purchase. Whether a vibrating alarm wristband store would be classified depends on how the site and copy position it — marketed as a silent alarm/gadget it is a general consumer product; marketed around hearing loss, sleep disorders or medication reminders it reads as "health-related products and services". The seller should check Events Manager → Datasets → Settings for a "Manage data source categories" section; if absent, no restricted category is currently assigned.
- Copy risk: ads addressed to the viewer's condition ("Hard of hearing?", "Can't hear your alarm?") are the classic personal-attributes pattern and are likely to be rejected or throttled; describing the product and the situation avoids it. (Based on general knowledge of the policy; the full policy text was not retrieved here.)
- For product selection this argues for broad, non-health self-purchase or household products, with no condition-specific claims on the site, so that neither the auction nor the data-source classification works against a small account.

### Gaps
- The full text of Meta's personal-attributes policy and of the 2025 announcement of health and wellness data restrictions (dates, which standard events are blocked in which tier) was not retrieved; tier descriptions beyond Meta's three-level summary come from vendors.
- No dataset quantifies how much a health-and-wellness classification raises cost per purchase.
- No direct measurement comparing CPM for a narrow health product versus a broad household/gift product from the same advertiser.
- Triple Whale's article on health and wellness brands under the restrictions could not be opened.

## 7. For a break-even cost per sale of about $28–$45: what CPM, click-through rate and conversion rate make the maths work

### Takeaway
Cost per purchase = CPM ÷ (1,000 × click-through rate × conversion rate). At a break-even of $28–$45 the seller needs roughly the ecommerce-median CPM (~$15) with a 1.5–2% link click-through rate and a 2–3.5% click-to-purchase rate; at the CPM seen in the November test (~$63) no realistic combination works (it would need about 2% CTR with a 7–11% conversion rate).

### Cited Findings
- Reference medians (Triple Whale, Aug 2025–Jul 2026): CPM $15.06, CTR 2.39%, conversion rate 1.53%, cost per acquisition $38.99. UNVERIFIED-A/B — [Triple Whale benchmarks](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks)
- BFCM 2025 reference (Triple Whale, 33,000+ shops): Meta CPM ~$22.26, cost per acquisition $34.06. UNVERIFIED-A — [Triple Whale BFCM 2025](https://www.triplewhale.com/bfcm-2025)
- Category cost per purchase medians: baby $29.87; lifestyle & boutique $32.04; apparel & accessories $37.41; pets $39.23; health & wellness $42.30; home & garden $48.61; electronics $51.06. UNVERIFIED-A — [Triple Whale benchmarks](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks)
- Meta: bid per impression = bid per estimated action × estimated action rate; costs are only stable after about 50 optimisation events. VERIFIED-META — [About bid cap](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/272503946776144); [Cost per optimization event](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/1707952432550214)

### Inferences
**The formula**
- Cost per click (CPC) = CPM ÷ (1,000 × CTR). Cost per purchase (CPA) = CPC ÷ conversion rate (CVR, purchases per link click).
- Shortcut with CTR and CVR written as percentages: **CPA = CPM × 10 ÷ (CTR% × CVR%)**. Check: $15 × 10 ÷ (1.5 × 2.22) = 150 ÷ 3.33 = $45.

**The seller's November test, worked through** (figures from the brief)
- CPM = £224 ÷ 4.564 thousand impressions = £49.08 (≈ $63.10).
- Link CTR = 63 ÷ 4,564 = 1.38%.
- CPC = £224 ÷ 63 = £3.56 (≈ $4.57).
- Conversion rate = 2 ÷ 63 = 3.17%.
- Cost per purchase = £224 ÷ 2 = £112 (≈ $144). Check with the shortcut: 631 ÷ (1.38 × 3.17) = 631 ÷ 4.37 = $144.
- That is 3.2 times the top of the break-even range (144 ÷ 45) and 5.1 times the bottom (144 ÷ 28).
- The CTR (1.38%) and conversion rate (3.17%) are not the problem on their face; the CPM is. But 2 purchases from 63 clicks is a tiny sample — the true conversion rate could plausibly be anywhere from well under 1% to around 10% — so the 3.17% should not be relied on.
- Holding that CTR and conversion rate, the CPM needed for a $45 cost per purchase is 45 × 4.37 ÷ 10 = **$19.70** (≈ £15.30), and for $28 it is 28 × 4.37 ÷ 10 = **$12.25** (≈ £9.50). The test CPM was 3.2 to 5.2 times too high.

**Cost per purchase grid** (CPA in $. How to read it: $28 or less works at any break-even in the range; between $28 and $45 works only if break-even is at the upper end; above $45 loses money)

| CPM | CTR 1.0%, CVR 2% | CTR 1.0%, CVR 3% | CTR 1.5%, CVR 2% | CTR 1.5%, CVR 3% | CTR 1.5%, CVR 4% | CTR 2.0%, CVR 2% | CTR 2.0%, CVR 3% | CTR 2.0%, CVR 4% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 50.00 | 33.33 | 33.33 | 22.22 | 16.67 | 25.00 | 16.67 | 12.50 |
| $15 | 75.00 | 50.00 | 50.00 | 33.33 | 25.00 | 37.50 | 25.00 | 18.75 |

**What CTR × CVR is required at each CPM** (required product = CPM ÷ (1,000 × target CPA))
- CPM $15: for $45 need CTR × CVR = 0.033% (e.g. 1.5% × 2.2%, or 1.0% × 3.3%); for $28 need 0.054% (e.g. 1.5% × 3.6%, or 2.0% × 2.7%).
- CPM $25: for $45 need 0.056% (e.g. 1.5% × 3.7%, or 2.0% × 2.8%); for $28 need 0.089% (e.g. 2.0% × 4.5%).
- CPM $40: for $45 need 0.089% (e.g. 2.0% × 4.4%); for $28 need 0.143% (e.g. 2.0% × 7.1%).
- CPM $63: for $45 need 0.140% (e.g. 2.0% × 7.0%, or 1.38% × 10.1%); for $28 need 0.225% (e.g. 2.0% × 11.3%).

**Reading the grid**
- The maths works comfortably only at CPM ≤ ~$15 with CTR ≥ 1.5% and CVR ≥ 3%, or at CPM ~$25 with CTR 2% and CVR 3–4%. A cold-traffic conversion rate of 3–4% on a $40 single product is above the ecommerce median (1.53% in the Triple Whale relay, though definitions may differ), so it needs a strong page and a product people buy on impulse.
- At CPM $40 only the best cell (2% CTR, 4% CVR) reaches $50 — still above break-even. Above roughly $35 CPM the product cannot work at this price point whatever the creative.
- The median brand's own numbers imply a cost per purchase of about $41 (150.6 ÷ (2.39 × 1.53) = $41.20, close to the reported $38.99) — inside the seller's range only at its top end. A $39.99 product with a $28 break-even is asking to beat the median established brand by about 30%.
- **Volume at $45/day:** at a $15 CPM the budget buys 3,000 impressions a day; at $63 it buys about 714 (45 ÷ 63 × 1,000). At a $40 cost per purchase that is about 1.1 purchases a day (≈ 8 a week) against Meta's ~50-a-week stability guideline, so results will be noisy for weeks even when the economics are sound. Decisions should be made on at least a few hundred link clicks, not on a few days.
- **Levers ranked by size for this seller:** (1) pick a product/audience where CPM is near the $12–$20 range rather than $60+ (a 3–4× effect); (2) raise price or order value — a bundle at $60–$70 would lift break-even proportionally; (3) conversion rate (landing page/offer), plausibly a 1.5–2× effect; (4) creative CTR, plausibly 1.5×; (5) seasonality — January is about 15–25% cheaper than October and 40–60% cheaper than the BFCM peak.

**What this means for launch timing and product choice** (synthesis across sections; inference)
- Do not scale spend from now until about mid-December: 23 Nov–1 Dec is the most expensive week of the year and the first half of December stays elevated. If spending at all, keep it minimal and use it to test creative, not to judge product viability.
- The cheapest window is 26 December to about the end of January. A sensible plan is to prepare creative and the product page in December and launch on or just after 26 December.
- Expect January to help by tens of percent, not multiples. A product that needs a 3× lower CPM (as the current one does) will not be rescued by timing.
- Prefer a product with: a broad audience (so the system can find cheap impressions); no health-condition positioning (to stay out of the dearest category and away from data-source restrictions and personal-attributes rejections); a self-purchase or household use case that fits January; either a price or bundle above $40 or a credible path to a 3%+ conversion rate; and creative that works as short vertical video so cheaper Reels/Stories inventory can be used.
- Categories with the lowest known cost per purchase (baby $29.87, lifestyle/boutique $32.04, apparel & accessories $37.41, pets $39.23) are the only ones whose *median* sits inside the $28–$45 range; home & garden ($48.61) and electronics ($51.06) medians sit above it and would need higher order values to work.

### Gaps
- Conversion-rate definitions differ between sources (per click, per session, per landing-page view), so the Triple Whale 1.53% may not be directly comparable with purchases ÷ link clicks.
- No benchmark for cost per purchase at a ~$40 order value specifically (Lebesgue's AOV-bucket table could not be opened).
- The break-even range ($28–$45) was taken from the brief and not checked against the seller's product cost, shipping, payment fees or refund rate.
- No data on expected CPM for a new ad account at $45/day in January for any specific product; the only way to know is a small controlled test after 26 December.
