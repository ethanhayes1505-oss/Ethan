## 1. Market size and growth: sleep tech, sleep aids, and the silent / vibrating alarm niche

### Takeaway
The headline "sleep tech" market figures (about $8-9 billion in the US for 2025, growing roughly 14% a year by one publisher's account) are real but nearly irrelevant to this product: they are dominated by trackers, smart beds and apnea devices. The vibrating-wristband niche itself is small, with roughly 7,000 Amazon US searches a week for the main keyword in mid-2026 (about 6% of the "alarm clock" head term), and it is sold mostly through Amazon at $20-45, where the leading listings each move an estimated 3,000-10,000 units a month.

### Cited Findings
**Broad market (scopes differ widely; do not add these together)**
- US sleep tech market: $7.79 billion in 2024, $8.89 billion in 2025, projected $25.74 billion by 2033 — [Straits Research](https://straitsresearch.com/report/sleep-tech-market/united-states)
- Global sleep tech devices market: $29.3 billion in 2025 — [Global Market Insights](https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/sleep-tech-devices-market); other publishers put 2025 global figures at roughly $24.7-25.3 billion (publisher not identified in the summary) — [The Insight Partners](https://www.theinsightpartners.com/reports/Sleep-Tech-Devices-Market)
- US "sleep health" market: nearly $2 billion in 2025, with "a slight dip in growth" — [Mintel](https://store.mintel.com/report/us-sleep-health-market-report). This is the only source found that reports slowing rather than accelerating growth.
- US sleep aids (over-the-counter, pharmacy scope): $2.10 billion in 2024, forecast growth 3.55% a year 2024-2029 (2024 figure, dated) — [Statista](https://statista.com/outlook/cmo/otc-pharmaceuticals/sleep-aids/united-states)
- US sleeping aid market: $5.2 billion in 2024, projected $10.1 billion by 2034, 6.8% a year (2024 figure, dated) — [Emergen Research](https://www.emergenresearch.com/index.php/industry-report/us-sleeping-aid-market)

- No Grand View Research, Precedence or Fortune Business Insights figure for US sleep tech surfaced in search.

**Underlying need (CDC, 2024 National Health Interview Survey, published April 2026)**

### Inferences
- Straits' US figures imply growth of about 14% a year from 2025 to 2033 (my calculation from $8.89 billion to $25.74 billion over eight years). Mintel's "slight dip in growth" on a narrower scope contradicts the rapid-growth story, so the honest summary is "large and probably growing, with publishers disagreeing by a factor of ten on size".
- The niche is tiny next to the category: 7,226 weekly searches against roughly 125,000 for "alarm clock" is about 6%, and the two figures come from different weeks. On Amazon the product is a known, searchable item; it is not a new category that needs explaining.
- If the leading sub-$30 wristband really sells 3,000-4,000 units a month at $23.88, that one listing takes roughly $70,000-95,000 a month (my arithmetic on AsinSight's estimates). That is evidence of real demand at about $24, and it is the price most searchers will see before or after clicking a $39.99 ad.
- Branded Amazon searches for FitSleeps (about 1,300 a week) suggest that direct-to-consumer ad spend in this niche leaks to Amazon: people see the ad, then search Amazon. A new brand with no Amazon listing would pay for demand that cheaper look-alikes then capture. The low conversion share of the top 3 products on the generic keyword (19.2% against 94.4% of clicks) is consistent with shoppers clicking the prominent listings and buying something cheaper, though I cannot confirm that reading.
- AsinSight's "top 10 average price" figures of $43.99 to $70.56 probably include smartwatches, Pavlok and other non-comparable items shown for the keyword, so they do not prove buyers pay $44 for a plain vibrating band.
- Homedics' low Walmart review counts after a year hint that a mainstream brand at the same $39.99 has not found fast sell-through at retail. This is weak evidence (one retailer, undated, Amazon counts unknown).

### Gaps
- No Amazon Best Seller Rank or first-hand review count for any vibrating band; Amazon could not be opened. AsinSight unit figures are modelled estimates with no stated method.
- No Google Trends data (direction or level) for "vibrating alarm", "silent alarm" or "alarm wristband"; no Google keyword-tool volumes.
- No market report sizes "vibrating alarm" or "wearable alarm" as a segment. Any dollar figure for the niche would be a guess.
- No sales or revenue data for FitSleeps, HiStrips or Homedics WakeBand.

## 2. Seasonality: when interest in alarm and sleep products peaks

### Takeaway
The only seasonality data found points to a December gift-driven peak for ordinary alarm clocks and a January peak for "better mornings" products such as sunrise clocks, with May-June the low; back-to-school and daylight-saving effects are asserted by sellers but not measured in anything I could find. On Meta, the gifting weeks raise the cost of reaching people by roughly half but lower the cost per order, because shoppers convert better.

### Cited Findings
- Alarm clocks (general): seasonal demand peaks in December, "largely gift-driven", with May the low month; a back-to-school dorm bump is mentioned in passing without data — [SaleHoo trends, 2025](https://www.salehoo.com/trends/alarm-clocks)
- Sunrise alarm clocks: peak demand "typically occurring in January", low in June — [SaleHoo trends, 2025](https://salehoo.com/trends/sunrise-alarm-clock)
- Dual alarm clocks: high month December, low month January — [SaleHoo trends, 2026](https://www.salehoo.com/trends/dual-alarm-clock)
- Meta costs in the Black Friday / Cyber Monday window: CPA $34.06 and CPM (cost per 1,000 impressions) $22.26, with CPM up about 7.8% on the prior year — [Triple Whale BFCM report](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-bfcm). Compare the 12-month averages of $38.99 CPA and $15.06 CPM — [Triple Whale](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks). The search summary described the BFCM figures as compared "with BFCM 2025", which cannot be right for a report available before 27 Nov 2026; the year of the BFCM data is unconfirmed (most likely BFCM 2025 against 2024).
- Daylight-saving research found used Google Trends to study sleep-related searches around clock changes, but in Italy (2015-2020) and about time of day, not purchase interest — [Journal of Circadian Rhythms, Nov 2023](https://doaj.org/article/25a363350dd947d38df6d2e66f05ecd0)
- Confirmed from the existing reports, not re-researched: 55% of adults felt tired after the spring clock change (AASM); premium sleep brands had gift ads live by 30 Oct to 6 Nov 2026; Black Friday is 27 Nov and Cyber Monday 30 Nov 2026.

### Inferences
- A wearable alarm sits between the two SaleHoo patterns: it is a gift ("for the snoozer") and a resolution product ("get up earlier"). The likely calendar is a gift peak from mid-November to about 18 December, a second peak in the first three weeks of January, a small bump around the March clock change, and a trough from May to July. This is inferred from adjacent products, not measured for wristbands.
- During BFCM, CPM was about 48% above the annual average while CPA was about 13% below it (my arithmetic on Triple Whale's figures, subject to the year caveat). For a store whose problem is cost per order, the next five weeks are the most forgiving test window of the year, and January results will look worse on conversion unless the resolution angle carries it.
- A test run in late November to December will flatter the product. A decision about whether paid social is viable year-round should not rest on gifting-season numbers alone.

### Gaps
- No month-by-month Google Trends curve for any alarm or sleep keyword; Trends could not be queried.
- No measured back-to-school (August) effect; the dorm claim is a seller-tool aside. The existing report's view that the student segment is August-only and skews to TikTok is unverified here.
- No US data on purchase or search changes around the March and November clock changes.
- SaleHoo is a seller-tool site with no stated method; its "peak month" labels are directional at best.

## 3. Buyer groups: size and strength of need

### Takeaway
Couples are the largest reachable group by an order of magnitude (roughly 70 million couple households, with 15% of adults already using a silent alarm for a partner), while the groups with the sharpest need (people with serious hearing loss, about 1.5% of adults, and night or evening shift workers, about 8% of employees) are small, already served by cheaper or more trusted products, and awkward to address in Meta copy. The largest group is also the one most likely to already own the free substitute: 37% of US adults, and nearly half of those under 50, have a smartwatch.

### Cited Findings
**Couples with different wake times**
- Married couples were 47% of US households in 2025; 39.7 million one-person households were 29% of all households — [Census Bureau, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/families-and-living-arrangements.html)
- About 9 million households were maintained by a cohabiting couple in 2020 (dated, pre-2025) — [Census Bureau](https://census.gov/library/stories/2021/06/unmarried-opposite-sex-couples-living-together-more-likely-than-married-couples-to-both-work.html)
- Confirmed from the existing report (AASM 2025, verified there against the PDF): 15% use a silent alarm for a bed partner (20% of men, 10% of women; 20-21% at ages 25-44); 37% change their bedtime; 31% sleep in another bed or room.

**Shift workers (this updates the existing report)**
- In 2024-25, about 15% of wage and salary workers usually worked a non-daytime schedule: roughly 5% evening shifts and 3% night shifts; 85% worked a regular daytime schedule; women 13% against men 16%. For comparison the 2017-18 edition put non-daytime work at 16% — [BLS Job Flexibilities and Work Schedules, released 24 Sep 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/flex2_09242026.htm)
- The existing report's "16.4% of 144.3 million" appears to be the 2017-18 edition, so it is dated; the new release resolves its "85% vs 83.6%" question in favour of 85% daytime for 2024-25.
- Utilities and wholesale and retail trade were more likely than other industries to have non-daytime shifts — [BLS, same release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/flex2_09242026.htm)

**Deaf and hard of hearing**
- About 15% of US adults (37.5 million) report some trouble hearing; this is from the 2012 National Health Interview Survey (dated, pre-2025) — [NIDCD quick statistics](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/statistics/quick-statistics-hearing)
- 2020 data: 12.3% of adults had some difficulty hearing even with a hearing aid, and 1.5% had a lot of difficulty or could not hear at all (dated, pre-2025) — [CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/71/wr/mm7112a5.htm)
- Disabling hearing loss is concentrated in older adults: about 5% at ages 45-54, 10% at 55-64, 22% at 65-74 and 55% at 75 and over — [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/statistics/quick-statistics-hearing)

**Heavy sleepers, students, new parents**
- 45% of adults did not wake up well-rested in 2024 (54.8% did) and 30.5% had short sleep — [CDC/NCHS Data Brief 559](https://cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db559.htm)
- From the existing report, not re-researched: about 25% of adults snooze "often" or "always" (YouGov 2023, dated); 3.6 million US births in 2024; about 2.8 million people in student housing in 2020 (dated).

**The substitute already on their wrists**
- 37% of US adults say they have a smartwatch (Feb 2026), and just under half of adults under 50 do — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/just-under-half-of-adults-under-50-have-a-smartwatch-while-smaller-shares-of-older-adults-say-so/)
- Nearly half of respondents own a wearable and 57% own at least one wearable or connected device (8,000 US adults, December 2025 fieldwork) — [Rock Health 2025 Consumer Adoption Survey](https://rockhealth.com/insights/whats-your-score-insights-on-wearables-and-connected-devices-from-rock-healths-2025-consumer-adoption-survey/)

### Inferences
- **Sizes (my arithmetic, approximate).** One-person households at 29% imply about 137 million households, so about 64 million married-couple households, plus roughly 9 million cohabiting (2020), for about 73 million couple households. Shift workers: 15% of roughly 144 million wage and salary workers (the base is the dated figure in the existing report) is about 21-22 million, of whom the evening and night group (8%) is about 11-12 million. Serious hearing loss: 1.5% of roughly 260 million adults is about 4 million.
- **Ranking by size:** couples (about 73 million households) > people who wake unrested or snooze habitually (a quarter to nearly half of adults, but a vague, low-intent group) > hard of hearing (37.5 million on the loose definition, about 4 million on the strict one) > non-daytime workers (about 21 million) > new parents (3.6 million births a year) > students in housing (about 2.8 million).
- **Ranking by strength of need (judgement, no willingness-to-pay data exists):** serious hearing loss (cannot hear an alarm at all) > night and early-shift workers (job at stake, sleeping household) > the woken partner in a couple (daily irritation, buys as a gift) > new parents (acute but brief) > heavy sleepers (strong need but the least likely to be satisfied by a wrist buzz, per the existing report's "slept through it" evidence) > students.
- **Why the strongest-need groups are poor Meta targets at $39.99.** The hearing-loss group skews well over 55, is loyal to bed shakers with tens of thousands of reviews at $30-42 (section 4), and cannot be addressed directly under Meta's personal-attributes rule (existing report). Shift workers are more likely than average to already wear a fitness tracker for work, though I found no data on that.
- **The couples segment is discounted by smartwatch ownership.** The core couples age band (25-44) is exactly where smartwatch ownership approaches half. A reasonable planning assumption is that 40-50% of the people an ad reaches in this group already own a device with a silent alarm, which leaves the "it's too weak / I don't want to wear my watch to bed / it's charging overnight" minority plus non-owners. That is still tens of millions of people, but it roughly halves the addressable share of every impression bought.
- This confirms the existing report's segment order (couples first) and adds two cautions it did not quantify: the shift-work figure it used is from 2017-18, and the "37.5 million hard of hearing" figure is from 2012 and overstates the group with a real need by about nine times.

### Gaps
- No willingness-to-pay or price-sensitivity data for any segment at $39.99.
- No Meta audience-size estimates were pulled (the existing report notes the same).
- No count of couples whose wake times actually differ; the AASM percentages are the nearest proxy.
- No current figure for total wage and salary workers in the 2024-25 BLS release (page blocked), and no nurse-specific shift data beyond the existing report's 3.28 million registered nurses.
- No data on how many smartwatch owners wear the watch overnight or find its alarm adequate; the existing report has only forum complaints.

## 5. Unit economics on paid social for a $30-60 impulse gadget

### Takeaway
The published benchmarks say the typical US e-commerce brand pays about $39 per order on Meta, and brands with an average order under $60 pay a median of about $43 and roughly break even on ad spend before product cost. This store's reported ad cost of about $47 per order is therefore close to normal for its price band, which means the shortfall is structural, not a sign of bad ads: at $28 profit on a single band the store needs a cost per order about 35% below the median brand in its bracket, or an average order of roughly $55-62.

### Cited Findings
- All-industry Meta benchmarks, 12 months to July 2026: CPA $38.99, CPM $15.06, conversion rate 1.53%, average order value $73.36, return on ad spend 1.88; CPA and CPM up year on year, conversion rate down about 5% — [Triple Whale](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks). A third-party summary of a different Triple Whale snapshot gives CTR 2.19%, CPM $14.19, CPA $38.19, ROAS 1.86, CVR 1.57%, unconfirmed on Triple Whale's own page — [Webtonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/e-commerce-facebook-ads-statistics)
- By order value: among 21 DTC brands with a Meta-attributed average order under $60, median CPA was $42.91 over the 12 months to 28 October 2026; at $60-80 (22 brands) it was $48.67; median first-purchase ROAS in the under-$60 band was about 1.03; the set covers 82 DTC brands — [MHI Growth Engine, AOV benchmarks](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/meta-ads-benchmarks-by-aov-2026/). The same site's vertical benchmark page cites 1,247 accounts and 2025 spend, so its sample descriptions differ between articles — [MHI Growth Engine](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/meta-ads-benchmarks-ecommerce-2026/)
- The same agency says that at low price points Meta CPA "regularly exceeds single-transaction profit", lists $25-45 as a typical single-unit order value and $65-100 with bundles as the viable target — [MHI Growth Engine, low-price DTC](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/meta-ads-for-low-price-dtc/)
- Conversion rate falls as order value rises (fashion: 2.18% under $50 down to 1.06% above $200), and acquisition cost generally rises with order value — [Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/meta-ads-benchmarks-by-industry-and-aov)
- Electronics on Meta: ROAS of 1.5-2.5 cited by two guides, and one source says DTC electronics brands pay a median CPA of $52-70 against $38 across all e-commerce. The search summary did not say which page carried the CPA claim; the candidates are [Madgicx](https://madgicx.com/labs/industries/electronics-tech-devices.md) and [Webtonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/phone-tech-retail-facebook-ads-statistics). Treat as unattributed.
- Trend in 2026: click-through rates at 12-month highs while conversion rates weaken; the piece is titled "Meta CVRs are tanking as CACs skyrocket" — [Northbeam, H1 2026 review](https://themediabuyer.northbeam.io/p/h1-2026-meta-cvrs-are-tanking-as-cacs-skyrocket). Another vendor puts Meta CPA at about $38, up about 38% — [ContentStudio](https://contentstudio.io/blog/meta-ads-benchmarks)
- Site conversion: the average Shopify store converts about 1.4% of visitors, and paid social traffic about 0.9%; other guides give paid social ranges of 0.8-1.2%, 1-2% and 0.9-2.1% — [EasySell](https://easysellapp.com/blogs/wiki/shopify-conversion-rate-benchmarks-2026-by-industry-ecommerce), [Blend Commerce](https://blendcommerce.com/blogs/shopify/ecommerce-conversion-rate-benchmarks-2026)
- Rules of thumb for the order value needed: "no universal minimum"; the test is order value times contribution margin against target acquisition cost plus fixed cost per order — [TopGrowth Marketing](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/dtc-unit-economics-guide/). Apparel and home goods "need to be profitable on every single order because repeat rates are low", unlike supplements and beauty — [Branvas](https://branvas.com/blogs/news/ecommerce-unit-economics-before-ads). One vendor gives a low-order-value acquisition cost range of $18-45 — [EightX](https://eightx.co/blog/average-cac-by-channel)
- This store's own numbers (from the existing upsell report, taken from the brief and not audited): band cost $8.33; profit $28 on a single band and $45 on the $64.99 2-pack; ad cost about £36 (roughly $47) per order; shortfall $10.50 per order.

### Inferences
- **The store is performing about as the benchmark predicts.** $47 per order against a median of $42.91 for brands under $60 is within about 10%. A new brand with no reviews, no email list and no retargeting pool landing near the median is not failing at ads; the price band is the problem.
- **Break-even arithmetic (mine, using the store's margins).** Profit of $28 on $39.99 and $45 on $64.99 is a contribution margin of about 70% before ads. Break-even ROAS is therefore about 1.43 (39.99 ÷ 28). The median brand under $60 achieves about 1.03. To cover a $42.91 cost per order at 70% margin needs an average order of about $61; at the all-industry $38.99 it needs about $56.
- **What that means for the 2-pack mix.** Each order's profit is $28 plus $17 for each buyer who takes two. To cover a $43 cost per order, about 88% of orders would need to be 2-packs; at $39, about 65%; at the BFCM-level $34, about 35%. The existing upsell report treats a 10-30 point shift to the 2-pack as the realistic range, so the 2-pack alone does not close the gap outside the gifting season. A 3-pack share would help but its profit was not given.
- **Sanity check on the funnel.** At a $15 CPM, a 2.2% click-through rate and a 1.5% conversion rate, 1,000 impressions yield about 0.33 orders, or about $45 per order (my arithmetic), which matches the benchmarks. At the 0.9% paid-social conversion rate reported for Shopify stores, the same clicks cost about $76 per order. A cold, unknown single-product store is more likely to sit near 0.9% than 1.5% until it has reviews and proof.
- **Why this category is harder than the averages.** There is no repeat purchase or subscription (the existing report confirms this), so lifetime value is roughly one order; the product must be profitable on the first sale, like apparel and home goods. The average benchmark brand has a $73 order value and returning customers; this store has neither.
- **Candid conclusion on $39.99.** On the evidence found, a $39.99 single band sold cold on Meta in the US is more likely than not to lose money on each order at benchmark costs. It becomes viable only if at least two of these hold: cost per order about a third below the price-band median (about $28 or less); average order value near $60 through 2-packs and add-ons; or a material share of sales coming from cheaper sources (organic social, email, Amazon) that Meta spend helps to feed.
- This agrees with the existing upsell report's conclusion ("ad cost and conversion remain the main problem") and adds the outside evidence that the store's ad cost is normal, not unusually bad.

### Gaps
- No benchmark is specific to sleep gadgets, vibrating bands or single-product stores; no conversion rate, order value or acquisition cost for FitSleeps, HiStrips or any competitor exists publicly.
- The Varos, Common Thread Collective and Shopify benchmark pages named in the brief did not surface; the under-$60 evidence rests on one agency's 21-brand sample that I could not open.
- No data on return and refund rates for this category, which matter because "slept through it" is the top complaint and long trials (FitSleeps' 100 days) raise refund exposure.
- The store's own figures are inconsistent across the existing reports (two orders in one place, 30 in another) and were not checked against the ad account in this pass.
- The triplewhale.com, mhigrowthengine.com, lebesgue.io and northbeam.io pages need to be allowed in the session's network settings before any of these numbers can be verified at source.

## 6. Channels: where similar products succeed and which is cheapest for a sub-$50 gadget

### Takeaway
The clearest demand evidence for this exact product is on Amazon, where buyers already search for it and the leading listings sell thousands of units a month at $20-45; Meta is where the direct-to-consumer brands spend (per the existing ad teardown), but nothing public shows they do so profitably. TikTok is reported to deliver orders at less than half Meta's cost per order ($17 against $39 in one dataset), which makes it the most plausible cheaper paid channel for a visual sub-$50 gadget, though the TikTok datasets contradict one another and none is specific to this category.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok ads, 2026: median e-commerce CPA about $17.07 and conversion rate 1.56%; CPA down 31.36% year on year while conversion rate fell 36.79% — [Triple Whale TikTok benchmarks](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/tiktok-benchmarks). Compare Meta's $38.99 from the same vendor — [Triple Whale](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks)
- TikTok electronics median CPA $31.25 on 2025 data, with most verticals under $22 — [Influee](https://influee.co/ca/blog/tiktok-ads-benchmarks)
- A second panel of 2,500-plus retail and e-commerce advertisers reports a TikTok median CPA of $3.78 and a 2.83% conversion rate; the agency reporting it notes the figures do not reconcile (a $0.26 cost per click at 2.83% implies $9.19) and that TikTok impressions cost about a third of Meta's but are clicked at about a quarter of Meta's rate — [TopGrowth Marketing](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/?p=46861)
- Qualitative comparison: TikTok has "lower CPM, sometimes lower CPA, but weaker ROAS" — [TrendTrack](https://www.trendtrack.io/blog-post/tiktok-vs-meta-cpm)
- Amazon: about 7,226 weekly US searches for "vibrating alarm wristband" (June 2026), a leading listing at about 10,000 monthly sales, and a $23.88 top seller at 3,000-4,000 units a month — [AsinSight](https://www.asinsight.com/report/US/vibrating-alarm-wristband), [AsinSight](https://www.asinsight.com/report/US/vibrating-watch)
- Mass retail: Homedics launched the WakeBand at Homedics.com, Amazon, CVS and Walmart at $39.99 in November 2025 — [HomePage News](https://www.homepagenews.com/home-housewares/homedics-launches-wakeband-vibrating-wristband-alarm/)
- TikTok Shop: no sold-unit figures for vibrating wristbands surfaced; the only TikTok Shop listing found was a sunrise alarm clock showing 249 sold — [TikTok Shop listing](https://www.tiktokshop.com/pdp/sunrise-alarm-clock-by-blonbar-dual-alarm-dimmable-night-light/1731235395980858264)
- From the existing reports, not re-researched: FitSleeps had about 1,379 active US Meta ads and HiStrips about 19,000 ads in total; a competitor's TikTok account targets "deep sleepers, students, nurses"; TikTok Shop prices for these bands reportedly run about $30-48; HiStrips runs search-optimised blog posts and an editorial pre-landing page.

### Inferences
- **Amazon is the proven channel for the product type; Meta is the proven channel for the brand type.** Generic bands win on Amazon at $20-25 with thousands of monthly sales. The direct-to-consumer brands (FitSleeps, HiStrips) spend heavily on Meta at $39-84 with aggressive discounts, long trials, shock-model upsells and paid add-ons, which suggests they need a much higher effective order value than $39.99 to make Meta pay. That is consistent with section 5.
- **Cheapest paid channel, on thin evidence:** TikTok looks cheaper per order than Meta (about $17-31 against about $39-43), and the product demonstrates well on video (the buzz on the wrist, the partner asleep). The caveats are serious: TikTok's conversion rate is falling fast, the datasets disagree by a factor of eight, lower-priced impulse items dominate TikTok's averages, and TikTok Shop takes a commission and pushes prices down (the existing report's $30-48 range).
- **Cheapest overall is probably unpaid:** creator seeding and organic short video cost product plus time, and the partner-alarm topic already travels on its own (the existing report documents viral posts and a 12,000-upvote story). I found no measured cost per order for seeding in this category, so this is a reasoned guess.
- **Google Shopping and Amazon capture existing intent** (about 7,000 weekly Amazon searches for the main keyword), so their conversion rates should be higher than cold social, but on both the band would sit beside $20-24 alternatives, where a $39.99 unknown brand with few reviews is at its weakest.
- **A workable channel mix for a small brand, as a hypothesis to test:** use Meta in the gifting window with the couples and 2-pack angles (where the existing reports found the least competition), test TikTok organic and Spark-style ads on the same creative, and judge both on profit per order, not on click costs. If Meta's cost per order stays near $43-47 into January, the evidence in sections 4 and 5 says to stop treating Meta as the primary channel at this price.
- **Leak risk:** without an Amazon listing, some of the demand that Meta ads create will be bought on Amazon from someone else (section 1). Whether to list there at $39.99 against $24 competitors is a separate decision this research cannot settle.

### Gaps
- No evidence at all on Google Shopping performance or costs for this product type.
- No influencer-seeding or affiliate cost data for sleep gadgets; no named creator campaigns for vibrating bands beyond the existing report's TikTok mention.
- No TikTok Shop sold counts, commission rates or price data for vibrating wristbands; TikTok Shop could not be opened.
- No channel-level revenue split for any competitor, so "FitSleeps succeeds on Meta" is unproven; heavy ad volume shows spending, not profit (the existing teardown makes the same point).
- No evidence comparing channels specifically for sub-$50 gadgets; the TikTok-against-Meta comparison is all-category.
