# Does "the sleep market" make Meta CPMs high? Category, audience, placement and competition evidence (US, 2025-2026)

Research date: 19 November 2026. Advertiser context: $39.99 vibrating silent-alarm wristband, US, Purchase-optimised, CPM about $70 in mid-November 2026, delivery mostly to women 45+ in Facebook feed.

**How to read the evidence labels.** In this session the page-fetch tool could not open ANY website (it failed on triplewhale.com, wordstream.com, lebesgue.io, webtonic.io, guptamedia.com, and also on www.facebook.com and www.hatch.co, which were supposed to be allowed). A network diagnostic was refused by the permission system, so I did not pursue it. Consequences:
- **"excerpt only"** = the figure comes from a search-engine excerpt or the search tool's summary of result pages. The page itself was NOT opened. Excerpts can be truncated, mis-attributed or out of context. Every web figure below is excerpt only unless marked otherwise.
- **"read in full (Meta connector)"** = full article text of a Meta Business Help Center page, returned by the Meta Ads connector's help-article tool (not a browser). These are the only sources read in full.
- No Reddit or X/Twitter page could be opened, and searches returned no Reddit/X posts at all (see the practitioner section).
- Not run (out of scope for web research, but available): the Meta Ads connector has account-level peer-benchmark tools (`ads_insights_industry_benchmark`, `ads_insights_performance_vs_peers`) that would give Meta's own comparison of this account's CPM against similar advertisers. That is the best primary evidence available and should be pulled by whoever is covering the account.

## 1. Category benchmarks: is health & wellness (or sleep) dearer than other categories on Meta?

### Takeaway
There is NO published benchmark for "sleep" as a Meta category; nothing found prices sleep products separately. For the broader "health & wellness" bucket, the largest dataset (Triple Whale) does show the highest median CPM of any ecommerce category, about $21.80 versus about $15 all-category, but cost per purchase for health & wellness is close to the overall median and BELOW electronics and home & garden. No dataset puts any category median anywhere near $70.

### Inferences
- On Triple Whale's numbers, health & wellness median CPM is about 45% above the all-category median ($21.80 vs $15.06, my arithmetic). On cost per purchase the gap is about 4% ($40.53 vs $38.99) and health & wellness is cheaper per purchase than electronics or home & garden. The higher CPM is offset by a higher click rate (3.02% CTR).
- A $70 CPM is roughly 3.2 times the highest category median found ($21.80) and about 2.5 times the most recent, most expensive panel median ($27.50). Category membership cannot account for that gap on any dataset found.
- A vibrating wristband alarm could equally be bucketed as electronics/wearables, home goods or gifts, whose medians ($14.67 to $15.09 CPM) sit at the all-category level. "Which category am I in" changes the benchmark by a few dollars, not by $50.
- Benchmarks disagree by a factor of two or more depending on who is in the sample (agency clients vs. platform-wide, median vs. mean, US vs. global), so only same-source comparisons are meaningful.

### Gaps
- No dataset reports "sleep" as its own category. No US-only CPM table by category for November 2026.
- No CPM, CPC or cost-per-purchase benchmark was found specifically for wearables or for gifts on Meta.
- Sample sizes for Triple Whale's category rows, Lebesgue and Superads were not visible in excerpts.
- Meta's own peer benchmark for this account was not pulled (see note at top).

### Takeaway
Large sleep-related advertisers exist and spend heavily overall (Somnigroup about $692M and Oura about $103M in their latest fiscal years), but I found NO figure for any sleep brand's Meta-specific spend in 2025-2026 and no Sensor Tower/Pathmatics or MediaRadar analysis of the sleep category. The claim "big sleep brands are outbidding you on Meta" is therefore plausible but unevidenced.

### Inferences
- The big sleep spenders sell $1,000+ mattresses, $350+ rings and $170+ clocks. They can afford far higher bids per purchase than a $39.99 product, so wherever Meta predicts the same person is likely to buy either, the wristband is structurally the weaker bidder. This is reasoning from price points, not measured auction data.
- Because broad delivery is per-person, the wristband's competitors for a woman aged 45+ in Facebook feed are every advertiser who values her, not only sleep brands (see sections 3 and 5).

### Gaps
- No Meta-specific spend for Hatch, Eight Sleep, Oura, Whoop, Manta, Loftie, Casper, Homedics, sleep-supplement or sleep-app brands for 2025-2026. Sensor Tower/Pathmatics and MediaRadar publish no free sleep-category breakdown that surfaced.
- No evidence found, for or against, that sleep brands specifically raise Meta spend for Q4 gifting; only general Q4 evidence exists.
- Brand sites (hatch.co etc.) could not be opened to check current holiday positioning.

## 3. Audience price: do women 45-64 / 65+ cost more per thousand impressions?

### Takeaway
There is practitioner evidence that OLDER audiences cost more per thousand than younger ones on Meta (about 2 times for 65+ vs 18-24 in one agency's data), and one dataset saying women cost more than men overall. There is NO public dataset of US CPM by age AND gender, and the one age-by-gender estimate found (modelled, European) shows older women slightly cheaper than older men. So "older" is supported; "older women specifically" is not established.

### Cited Findings
- Agency data (Kevin Goodwin newsletter, 3 Oct 2024, OLDER): the 65+ group is "2x more expensive than the 18-24 bracket and 80% more expensive than the 25-34 bracket"; attributed to older users being highly engaged "clickers" and holding large buying power. Excerpt only. — [Performance Marketing Reboot 10.3.24](https://kevingoodwin.substack.com/p/performance-marketing-reboot-10324)
- Same author, one sample account (28 Aug 2024, OLDER): 65+ CPM was 6x the 25-34 cohort with 3x the click-through rate; applying bid multipliers against 55+ moved the 35-44 share of spend from 18% to 40% and roughly halved CAC. Single account. Excerpt only. — [Performance Marketing Reboot 8.28.24](https://kevingoodwin.substack.com/p/performance-marketing-reboot-82824)
- Lebesgue ("2025 update"): average CPM for ads targeting women $6.01 vs men $4.92 (about 22% higher); country and age not specified; November is the highest month in its series. Excerpt only. — [Lebesgue CPM by gender](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/facebook-cpm-by-gender)
- AdSpendSpy modelled European estimates (not Meta prices): women 55-64 EUR 6.40 vs men EUR 6.60; women 65+ EUR 5.90 vs men EUR 6.10. The same source says Meta publishes no US breakdown. Excerpt only; modelled; not US. — [AdSpendSpy](https://www.adspendspy.com/facebook-cpm-by-country)
- A benchmark piece describes women 25-44 in North America and Western Europe as typically the highest-CPM segment (unsourced claim). Excerpt only. — [Influee good CPM](https://influee.co/blog/good-cpm-facebook-ads)
- Reporting on Meta ad load (June 2025): Meta's revenue is driven by Gen X with higher purchasing power; 18-24s see roughly half the ad exposure of older users; Meta shows more ads to users more likely to buy. Excerpt only. — [Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/tech/25/06/45859835/not-gen-z-metas-ad-revenue-driven-by-gen-x-with-higher-purchasing-power-report); [Business Insider NL](https://www.businessinsider.nl/gen-x-has-become-metas-biggest-gold-mine/)
- Jon Loomer: Meta often pushes lead-campaign budget to 65+ because those results are cheap; he recommends value rules or bid adjustments rather than assuming the skew is right. Excerpt only. — [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/qvt/low-quality-leads-and-age-distribution/)
- Meta's own wording on audience price: "a highly niche or specific audience with high-income demographics typically costs more to reach due to increased competition for their attention" and "more advertisers bidding for the same audience will drive up the price". Read in full (Meta connector). — [The cost of Instagram ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/841206208740012)
- Who else bids for 65+ in mid-November: Medicare open enrollment runs 15 October to 7 December, with plan marketing allowed from 1 October; KFF counted nearly 650,000 TV airings of Medicare ads in nine weeks of one season. A CCDH investigation estimated Meta earned $14.3M from Medicare scam advertisers, with 73% of the top scammers' impressions going to people over 65. Neither source gives CPMs. Excerpt only. — [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/report/how-health-insurers-and-brokers-are-marketing-medicare/); [All About Cookies on CCDH](https://allaboutcookies.org/facebook-medicare-scam-ads)

### Inferences
- Mid-November is the one window when insurers and brokers are legally in-market for the 65+ audience, which overlaps the upper end of "women 45+". It is reasonable to suspect extra auction pressure on 65+ impressions then, but no source measures it; treat as hypothesis.
- If 65+ impressions cost about 2x the youngest bracket (one agency, 2024) and delivery is concentrated there, age mix alone could plausibly explain a large part of an above-benchmark CPM. The account's own age-by-gender breakdown is the only way to confirm.
- Higher CPM for older users is not automatically bad: the same sources report much higher click rates. What matters is cost per purchase by age, which Meta's guidance (section 5) warns can mislead when read per segment.

### Gaps
- No US dataset of Meta CPM by age and gender for 2025-2026. No evidence specific to women 45-64.
- No measured CPM effect of Medicare, finance or retail advertisers on older audiences in Q4.

## 4. Placement price: is Facebook feed dearer than Reels, Stories and Instagram? How do static images fare in Reels?

### Takeaway
Most placement tables put feed above Reels and Stories on CPM (Reels roughly 20-50% cheaper), but on the best sample found Instagram as a whole is dearer than Facebook (median $31.15 vs $20.30 CPM). So Facebook feed is the expensive placement within Facebook, not the most expensive place on Meta. No study isolating static images in Reels was found.

### Cited Findings
- MHI Media, 48 DTC brands, 8 June to 13 July 2026: median CPM $31.15 on Instagram vs $20.30 on Facebook (Instagram 53% higher); link CTR 1.78% vs 1.49%; link CPC $1.93 vs $1.48; middle half of brands on Instagram CPM $18.24 to $52.94. Cannot split purchases by placement. Excerpt only. — [MHI Facebook vs Instagram](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/facebook-vs-instagram-cpm-benchmarks-2026/)
- MHI earlier post (February 2026, Q1 placement averages, different sample): Facebook Feed about $12-16 CPM, Facebook Reels about $7-11, Instagram Feed about $18-24, Instagram Reels about $11-15. Excerpt only. — [MHI Facebook vs Instagram ads](https://mhigrowthengine.com/blog/facebook-ads-vs-instagram-ads/)
- Influee 2026 ranges (dataset not stated): Facebook Feed $8-14, Instagram Feed $7-12, Facebook Stories $5-9, Instagram Stories $5-9, Facebook Reels $4-8. Excerpt only. — [Influee](https://influee.co/blog/good-cpm-facebook-ads)
- Lebesgue placement analysis (2024 data, OLDER): Facebook had the highest CPM through 2024 at $12.15; Rewarded Video, Facebook Stories and News Feed had the highest CPMs (conflicts with sources that rank Stories cheap). Excerpt only. — [Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/latest-facebook-ad-cpm-benchmarks)
- Other trackers: Instagram Reels about $4.29 CPM vs Stories about $7.25; WebFX Facebook $7.47 CPM and $1.06 CPC, Instagram $6.25 to $7.68; Emplifi Q2 2026 Facebook Feed $3.60 (global, brand-side data). These are far below DTC purchase-campaign levels. Excerpt only. — [WebFX](https://www.webfx.com/blog/social-media/meta-benchmarks/); [Emplifi Q2 2026](https://emplifi.io/resources/q2-2026-ecommerce-worldwide-benchmarks/)
- Static images in Reels: no placement-isolated study found. Closest: a 2022 Instagram test reported by TechCrunch found feed posts converted impressions to link clicks at 7.1% vs 1.2% for the compared placement (OLD, not static-specific). One blog cites Meta as saying campaigns mixing static and video achieved conversion lift at a 17% higher rate than static-only (attribution to Meta not verified). One vendor argues a repurposed feed image in Reels gets the lower CPM but performs badly (untested claim). Excerpt only. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/12/15/which-instagram-ad-placement-is-more-cost-effective-reels-feed-posts-or-stories/); [AdLibrary placements](https://adlibrary.com/posts/placement-in-advertising); [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/meta-ads-vertical-video-formats-452902)
- Meta on reading per-placement costs: the delivery system aims for "the most optimization events ... at the lowest average cost overall - not the lowest average cost for each placement"; worked example where turning off the dearer-looking placement raises overall cost from $3.00 to $3.25 per event; "one placement's average cost per optimization event being higher than another's doesn't necessarily mean it's inefficient." Read in full (Meta connector). — [How the Meta delivery system works using Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/965529646866485)
- Meta: Sales, Leads and App promotion objectives "now default to Advantage+ placements and the option to make placement selections is removed in ad sets" (gradual rollout); experiment on 147k campaigns (30 Aug to 7 Sept 2025) showed 11.7% lower CPA for Advantage+ placements vs manual. Read in full (Meta connector). — [About Advantage+ placements](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/196554084569964)

### Inferences
- If the advertiser's creative is static images only, delivery will naturally concentrate in feed, where static images fit and where older Facebook users spend time; Reels inventory (the cheap inventory in every table) is harder to win with a still image. This is consistent with the delivery pattern described but is my reasoning, not a measured result.
- Manual removal of Facebook feed may no longer be possible for a Sales campaign, per Meta's rollout note, and Meta's own example argues against it anyway.

### Gaps
- No US, 2026, placement-level CPM table from a large named dataset; no data on static-image performance in Reels placements.

## 5. Does Meta price by "interest category" when targeting is broad? How delivery actually picks people

### Takeaway
No. Meta's own documentation describes a per-impression auction between every ad eligible for that person. There is no category price list. The product category affects cost only indirectly: through which people Meta predicts will convert (so where the budget goes) and through which other advertisers are bidding for those same people.

### Cited Findings (all read in full via Meta connector unless noted)
- "An auction takes place whenever someone is eligible to see an ad." "When there's an opportunity to show someone an ad, the ads with a target audience that the person belongs to are eligible to compete in the auction." A person can fall into multiple advertisers' audiences. — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- "The winner of the auction is the ad with the highest total value, subject to a price floor (minimum price)." Total value combines three factors: bid; estimated action rates ("the probability that showing an ad to a person leads to that desired outcome of the advertiser"); and ad quality. "An ad that's more relevant to a person could win an auction against ads with higher bids." — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- Meta "may use data about you and your ad account to make adjustments that could affect auction outcomes and prices", including to "account for regional and other unique business costs", but such adjustments "will not cause us to charge you more than your bid". — [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542)
- "Bid per impression = bid per estimated action x estimated action rate." — [About bid cap](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/272503946776144)
- "All ads across Meta technologies such as Facebook compete against each other in this process." — [Ad auction (glossary)](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/163066663757985)
- Advantage+ audience: Meta's AI "uses numerous pieces of information to find the audience ... for example: past conversions, Meta Pixel data, interactions with previous ads"; models "analyze millions of signals to show your ads to people who are likely to take action". Meta's claimed result: 7.2% lower cost per result for sales/app objectives (meta-analysis of 469 A/B studies, Jan 2023 to Aug 2024). — [About Advantage+ audience](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/273363992030035)
- Audience suggestions are not limits: "We'll show ads to people matching your suggestion, and to other audiences when it's likely to improve performance." Hard controls are location, minimum age, excluded custom audiences and languages. — [How to create a campaign using Advantage+ audience](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/793748385630490)
- Meta's troubleshooting for high costs: "we show your ads to the people we think are most likely to take action at the lowest cost"; "We don't recommend removing an audience segment just because it has shown higher costs to date, because the delivery system seeks the lowest cost opportunities first"; "we subsidize relevant ads in the ad auction, so more relevant ads often cost less"; if costs stay high "you might be facing increased auction competition". — [Troubleshoot ad performance: your costs are too high](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/2727273724224874)
- Meta's list of what moves ad cost: target audience, industry competition ("more advertisers bidding for the same audience will drive up the price"), ad relevance and quality, time and seasonality ("ad costs often spike during peak shopping seasons like Black Friday, Cyber Monday, and the holidays"), bidding strategy, and objective ("driving a direct conversion often costs more than a simple brand awareness campaign"). — [The cost of Instagram ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/841206208740012)
- Third-party descriptions of the formula differ (bid x estimated action rate + ad quality, vs. all three multiplied); Meta publishes no exact formula. Excerpt only. — [Dotidot](https://www.dotidot.io/post/meta-ads-auction-how-facebook-decides-which-ads-win); [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/qvt/how-to-win-the-meta-ad-auction/)

### Inferences
- With Purchase optimisation, Meta bids more per impression for people with a higher predicted purchase probability. A high CPM can therefore mean Meta is deliberately buying a small number of expensive, high-probability impressions (older women in feed), not that a "sleep" surcharge exists. Whether that is efficient shows up in cost per purchase, not CPM.
- "The sleep market" enters only two ways: (a) sleep-related creative attracts and converts a particular kind of person, which trains delivery toward that demographic; (b) other advertisers who want those same people set the price. Both are about the person, not the label.
- Because ad quality and estimated action rate are in the auction, weak engagement or a poor post-click experience raises the effective price independently of category.

### Gaps
- Meta does not disclose how the price floor or "auction adjustments" are set, so their contribution to any one account's CPM cannot be measured from outside.

## 6. Sleep-specific demand timing, and are ad costs lower in January?

### Takeaway
Search and sales signals for sleep products point to a January (New Year) peak, with December strong for gift-type items; evidence for alarm clocks specifically is thin. Meta CPMs are widely reported to fall after the holidays, with one multi-year US analysis putting January-February about 20% below the yearly average and one roundup showing January about 39% below November.

### Cited Findings
- Google Trends study (preprint): searches for melatonin, magnesium, zinc and similar sleep supplements peaked 28 January to 3 February 2024. Excerpt only. — [Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-8148926/v1)
- Sleep Foundation 2023 survey reporting: melatonin searches jumped in each of five winters, peaking each January. OLDER. Excerpt only. — [The Well News](https://www.thewellnews.com/health/winter-has-consumers-reaching-for-melatonin-other-sleep-aids/)
- "Sleeping mask" search index peaks: December 2025 (98) and January 2025 (90); smart-ring Amazon sales surged in January 2026 and December 2025. Vendor trend report, methodology unverified. Excerpt only. — [Accio](https://www.accio.com/business/trending-sleep-products)
- Alarm clocks: a dropship supplier's trend page lists December as the high month and May as the low. No methodology. Excerpt only. — [SaleHoo](https://www.salehoo.com/trends/alarm-clocks)
- Daylight saving: a 2023 Journal of Circadian Rhythms study (Italy, 2015-2020) found search behaviour shifts around clock changes and included "alarm clock" in a sleep/health term group, but no monthly alarm-clock pattern was reported in the excerpts. Not US. Excerpt only. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/25a363350dd947d38df6d2e66f05ecd0)
- Back to school: Google's analyses show school-related searches start as early as May and peak in August, for back-to-school terms generally, not alarm clocks. OLD (2011 onward). Excerpt only. — [Google Search blog](https://search.googleblog.com/2011/09/search-insights-lesson-for-back-to.html)
- January ad costs: an analysis of 2021-2023 US Facebook campaigns reports costs peaking about 40% above the yearly average in the holidays and dipping about 20% below it in January and February (OLDER data; likely Birch/Revealbot, attribution not confirmed). Excerpt only. — [Birch](https://bir.ch/blog/facebook-advertising-costs/)
- US monthly CPM roundups (origin of the series unclear): one says November 2025 peaked at $25.22 before falling to January lows; another says US CPM peaked at $28.09 in November and ended January 2026 at $17.12. Excerpt only; which page carried which figure could not be confirmed. — candidates: [Affect Group](https://affectgroup.com/blog/meta-ads-cpm-in-the-united-states-benchmarks-for-facebook-and-instagram/); [AdManage](https://admanage.ai/blog/facebook-cpm-benchmarks-by-industry)
- Black Friday week 2025 (Lebesgue): the median brand paid 59% more per thousand impressions than its own September average (84% more on Black Friday itself), while cost per purchase FELL 20% and Meta-reported ROAS rose 19%. By category the CPM rise was +116% for home & decor but only +42% for "beauty, health & supplements". Excerpt only; the 116% figure's time frame is ambiguous in the excerpt. — [Lebesgue Black Friday](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/are-meta-ads-worth-it-black-friday); secondary [NA Media Experts](https://www.namediaexperts.com/blog-posts/black-friday-q4-ad-cost-benchmarks-2026-cpm-cpc-by-channel)
- Triple Whale BFCM 2025: Meta CPM about $22.26, up about 7.8% on 2024 (dataset: $606.6M ad spend, 26M orders). A third-party snapshot gives $20.33 (+17.79%). Excerpt only. — [Triple Whale BFCM Facebook](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-bfcm); [Ecorn](https://ecorn.agency/blog/facebook-black-friday-ads)
- Mid-November 2026 movement: Electronics & Gaming saw a short CPM spike in the week of 10 November (+26%) that then eased; Toys & Hobbies +29% in the latest week (small sample). Excerpt only. — [Supermetrics Black Friday playbook](https://supermetrics.com/ebook/black-friday-retail-playbook)
- Counterpoint: Jon Loomer found, for one holiday season in his own account, more competition but no automatic CPM increase, because impressions also rise. Excerpt only. — [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/qvt/holiday-competition-and-cpm/)
- "Q5" (late December into January) is used by media buyers for cheaper inventory. Excerpt only; no figures in excerpt. — [Gupta Media Q5](https://www.guptamedia.com/insights/q5-marketing-holiday-advertising); [Marketing Brew](https://marketingbrew.com/stories/media-buyers-navigating-higher-ad-prices-on-meta)

### Inferences
- January combines the best-evidenced sleep-interest peak with the cheapest Meta inventory of the year. If the wristband's buyer is motivated by New Year routine change rather than gifting, January is the more favourable window on both counts.
- The health bucket shows a smaller Black Friday CPM lift (+42%) than home & decor (+116%), which cuts against the idea that health/sleep audiences become unusually expensive in Q4; the seasonal premium appears larger for gift-heavy home categories.
- Lebesgue's finding that CPM rose 59% while cost per purchase fell 20% shows a high Q4 CPM can coexist with better economics when conversion rates rise. That only helps a product people are buying as a gift or on promotion.

### Gaps
- No US sales seasonality data for alarm clocks or vibrating/silent alarms; no US daylight-saving evidence; no evidence on back-to-school demand for alarms.
- No source reports ad costs for sleep products specifically by month.

## Overall answer to the hypothesis ("the sleep market itself makes Meta CPMs high")

### Takeaway
Not supported. No evidence was found that the sleep category is priced higher on Meta, and Meta's own documentation describes no category pricing at all. The nearest supported statement is that the broader health & wellness bucket has the highest median CPM in one large dataset (about $21.80, roughly 45% above the all-category median), with cost per purchase about average. That is far short of explaining $70. The evidence points instead to who the impressions are going to and when: US inventory (the dearest market, prices up 23% year on year), mid-November seasonality, an older audience (about 2x younger brackets in one agency's data), Facebook feed rather than Reels, and Purchase optimisation that bids up for high-probability buyers.

### Cited Findings
- See sections 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6; key sources: [Triple Whale](https://www.triplewhale.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks), [About ad auctions](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/430291176997542), [Kevin Goodwin](https://kevingoodwin.substack.com/p/performance-marketing-reboot-10324), [Lebesgue Black Friday](https://lebesgue.io/facebook-ads/are-meta-ads-worth-it-black-friday), [PPC Land on Meta Q3 2026](https://ppc.land/meta-takes-9-89bn-teen-settlement-charge-as-ad-revenue-gains-26/)

### Inferences
- The factors above multiply rather than add, so a stack of ordinary premiums (US, Q4, 65+ skew, feed, purchase bidding, small new account with limited signal) can plausibly reach $70 without any sleep-specific effect. This is a reasoned explanation, not a measured decomposition; the account's own breakdowns by age, gender and placement are needed to confirm it.
- What "can be avoided" on this evidence: timing (January), geography (test UK/Canada/Australia with realistic expectations on CPA), creative format (video suited to Reels to access cheaper inventory), and ad relevance (Meta says relevant ads are subsidised). What cannot be avoided: there is no setting that opts out of competing with every other advertiser who wants the same person.

### Gaps
- Everything from the open web is excerpt only; none of the benchmark pages were read in full. Figures should be re-checked against the source pages before being quoted to the owner as exact.
- The single most decisive evidence, Meta's peer benchmark and the account's own age/gender/placement breakdown, was not part of this web research.
